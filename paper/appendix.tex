Several patterns emerge.  \emph{All four archetypes} peak late in FG3A (three-point attempt rate) and DBPM (defensive box plus/minus), consistent with the finding in Section~\ref{sec:uncovering_metrics} that these are skill-acquisition metrics that develop with experience regardless of player type.  The archetypes diverge most sharply on athleticism metrics (BLK, OREB, STL, USG) and on overall production ceiling.  Reading the cluster assignments directly from Figure~\ref{fig:latent_archetypes}:

The most telling cross-positional grouping is the archetype that unites elite playmaker-scorers across all five traditional positions.  A positional-dummy approach would never cluster a pass-first center alongside a perimeter ball-handler; the jointly learned latent embedding does so because both share the same multi-metric aging structure --- creative playmaking, high offensive efficiency (OBPM), and durable late-peaking production --- rather than physical role.

Beyond peak timing and peak value, the archetypes also differ in the \emph{rate} of career ascent and decline.  For each posterior draw, we compute the mean pre-peak first derivative (the instantaneous rate of improvement from career start to peak age) and the mean post-peak first derivative (the instantaneous rate of decline from peak to career end), using the analytically derived GP first derivative at each observed age.  Figure~\ref{fig:archetype_slopes} summarises these by archetype and metric.  Figure~\ref{fig:archetype_slope_ratio} shows the asymmetry ratio (pre-peak slope divided by absolute post-peak slope): values greater than 1 indicate metrics for which the ascent to peak is slower than the subsequent decline, while values less than 1 indicate faster ascent than decline.  Archetypes with systematically higher asymmetry on athleticism metrics (BLK, OREB) and lower asymmetry on skill metrics (OBPM, AST) reflect the distinction between physical and cognitive aging: athleticism metrics are slow to develop and fast to erode, whereas skill metrics accumulate more rapidly and decline more gradually.

\subsection{Modality-Specific Archetype Clustering}
Each modality-specific embedding is derived from the bilinear structure of the model.  For a given modality $m$ (peak timing, peak value, or curvature basis $m$), the player-metric effect is $X_p^\top \boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k,(m)}$, where $\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k,(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^q$ is the per-metric weight vector drawn from the posterior.  We rescale each latent dimension $j$ of $X_p$ by the posterior standard deviation of $\gamma^{k,(m)}_j$ across all $K$ metrics, forming the modality-specific coordinate
\[
  \tilde{X}_{pj}^{(m)} \;=\; X_{pj} \cdot \mathrm{std}_{k}\!\bigl(\gamma^{k,(m)}_j\bigr).
\]
This operation siphons the learned feature importance from the weight matrix into the latent coordinates: a dimension $j$ whose weight varies substantially across metrics is strongly modality-discriminating and is amplified; a dimension whose weight is nearly constant across metrics contributes uniformly to all metrics and is suppressed.  The raw archetype clustering of Section~\ref{sec:latent_space} uses $X_p$ directly, treating all latent dimensions symmetrically and remaining agnostic to any particular facet of the career arc.

The rescaled coordinates $\tilde{X}^{(m)}$ are Procrustes-aligned across posterior draws and subjected to the same Ward hierarchical clustering.  Figures~\ref{fig:modality_pca}, \ref{fig:modality_k_diagnostics}, and \ref{fig:modality_dendrograms} show the PCA, cluster-selection diagnostics, and Ward dendrograms for the peak-timing ($\boldsymbol{\gamma}^k_{\text{age}}$) and peak-value ($\boldsymbol{\gamma}^k_{\text{val}}$) modalities.  Curvature-based modalities (HSGP basis weights $\beta^k_m$, $m=1,\ldots,M$) are shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:latent_appendix}.  The peak-timing modality groups players by \emph{when} their metrics peak; the peak-value modality groups by \emph{how high} they peak.

\subsubsection{Latent Embedding Prior}

Let
\[
  Z_p = \bigl[-\log d_p,\; h_p,\;\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{pos}_p = F],\;\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{pos}_p = G]\bigr]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^4
\]
collect four player-level covariates: negative log draft position $-\log d_p$ (a proxy for scouting consensus), height $h_p$, and two indicator variables for positional group (Forward and Guard, with Center as the baseline category).  Players without a recorded draft position (undrafted free agents) are assigned a sentinel rank of $P{+}1$, where $P$ is the total number of players in the cohort, so that $-\log d_p$ defaults to the minimum value after standardization; survival model entry times for such players are set to their first observed NBA season.  We introduce a learned projection $W \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times q}$ with hierarchical scale $\sigma_W$ and a residual scale $\sigma_X < 1$, and place the prior
\begin{align}
\label{eq:structured_prior}
  X_p &\sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(Z_p^\top W,\; \sigma_X^2\, I_q\bigr), \quad
  W \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, \sigma_W^2\, I_{4\times q}), \nonumber\\
  \sigma_W &\sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(1), \quad
  \sigma_X \sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(0.5).
\end{align}
The prior mean $Z_p^\top W$ pulls a player's latent position toward the subspace predicted by draft standing, physical size, and positional role; the residual scale $\sigma_X$ (concentrated below 1) controls how tightly the data must pull $X_p$ away from that prior mean.  When $\sigma_W \to 0$ the prior reduces to $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_X^2 I_q)$, recovering the isotropic case.  Crucially, $W$ is shared across all players, so the projection is learned from the full roster rather than treated as fixed. 

\subsection{Career Duration Formulation}
\label{sec:survival_linear_map}
A concentration greater than one encodes an increasing hazard—players become progressively more likely to exit as they age, consistent with age-related attrition. 
Because player $p$ is only observed after season $\alpha_p$, the exit-time likelihood must be left-truncated at $\alpha_p$.  For a Weibull with cumulative hazard
\begin{equation}
    H(t;\,\eta_p(X_p),\gamma_p(X_p)) = \left(\frac{t}{\eta_p(X_p)}\right)^{\gamma_p(X_p)},
\end{equation}
the truncated log-likelihood contributions are:
\begin{equation}
    \ell_p =
    \begin{cases}
        \log h(T_p;\,\eta_p(X_p),\gamma_p(X_p)) - \bigl[H(T_p;\,\eta_p(X_p),\gamma_p(X_p)) - H(\alpha_p;\,\eta_p(X_p),\gamma_p(X_p))\bigr] & \text{(observed)}, \\
        -\bigl[H(T_p;\,\eta_p(X_p),\gamma_p(X_p)) - H(\alpha_p;\,\eta_p(X_p),\gamma_p(X_p))\bigr] & \text{(censored)},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:surv_lik}
\end{equation}
where $h(t;\,\eta,\gamma) = (\gamma/\eta)(\,t/\eta)^{\gamma-1}$ is the Weibull hazard.
Both parameters are linked to the latent embedding $X_p \in \mathbb{R}^q$ via a bilinear projection shared with the performance likelihood:
\begin{align}
    \tilde{\eta}_p   &= \frac{\sigma_{\text{exit}}}{\sqrt{q}}\,X_p^\top \psi_{\text{exit}}, \qquad
    \eta_p = \exp\!\bigl(\mu_{\text{scale}} + \tilde{\eta}_p\bigr), \label{eq:eta} \\
    \tilde{\gamma}_p &= X_p^\top \psi_{\text{rate}} + \delta_0,\qquad
    \gamma_p = 1 + 2\,\sigma(\tilde{\gamma}_p), \label{eq:gamma}
\end{align}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function, $\mu_{\text{scale}} \sim \mathcal{N}(\log 11.5,\, 0.5)$ is a global log-scale offset, $\sigma_{\text{exit}} \sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(0.5)$ controls the player-specific spread in career length, and $\delta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\log 10,\, 0.5)$ is a global offset on the log-odds of the concentration.  The log-linear scale places the prior central career length near 11.5 seasons while allowing the latent embedding to shift $\eta_p$ multiplicatively; the sigmoid on $\tilde{\gamma}_p$ constrains $\gamma_p \in (1, 3)$, encoding the prior belief that career-exit hazard is increasing.  The latent embedding thereby links career longevity to the same low-dimensional player representation that drives performance.

\subsection{Model Validation}
\label{sec:appendix_validation}

The four holdout schemes are implemented by selecting a random 20\% subset of players and masking their observations as follows.

\begin{description}
  \item[Hold-out Last $k$ ($k{=}2$, 727 obs).] The final 2 observed seasons of each selected player are excluded from the training likelihood. The model therefore sees the player's career up to but not including their most recent 2 seasons, then is scored on those withheld seasons. This is the most operationally relevant scheme: it mimics the real task of forecasting a player's next 1--2 seasons from their career to date.

  \item[Hold-out First $k$ ($k{=}2$, 757 obs).] The first 2 observed seasons of each selected player are withheld. The model sees seasons 3 onward and must recover the player's rookie and sophomore output. This tests whether the concave prior and latent embedding generalise backward in time and whether the model is well-calibrated for players with sparse early-career histories.

  \item[Random Interior (413 obs).] For each selected player, a random 20\% of their interior seasons (all but the first and last) are withheld. Unlike the last-$k$ and first-$k$ schemes, the model has observations on both sides of every gap, so this tests \emph{interpolation} rather than extrapolation. It simulates partial seasons due to injury or data censoring.

  \item[Hold-out Peak (450 obs).] The single season of peak observed production for each selected player is withheld. This directly evaluates whether the model's explicit peak parameterisation ($t^*$, $f^*$) allows it to recover the peak even when it is unobserved, which is the hardest interpolation task and the one most sensitive to misspecification of the concave prior.
\end{description}

\subsection{Latent Dimension Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation_q}

To select the latent dimension $q$ we fit the concave model under the hold-out last-$k$ scheme for $q \in \{5, 10, 15, 20\}$, holding all other hyperparameters fixed.  Tables below report holdout RMSE (with bias) and average log-loss for each value of $q$, separately for each of the four holdout schemes.

$q = 10$ achieves the best mean holdout RMSE (0.924) and mean log-loss (3.99) across all 17 metrics, winning on 6 metrics by RMSE and 11 metrics by log-loss.  Moving from $q = 5$ to $q = 10$ yields the largest improvements on OBPM, DREB, and MPG, suggesting the 5-dimensional space is too compressed to capture the variance in these metrics.  Larger values ($q = 15, 20$) do not improve predictive accuracy — mean RMSE rises to 1.016 and 0.968 respectively — and $q = 20$ wins zero metrics by log-loss, indicating degraded distributional calibration at higher dimension.  We therefore adopt $q = 10$.

\label{sec:convergence}

Figure~\ref{fig:log_post_trace} shows the log joint posterior trace across 50 post-burn-in draws for each chain.  The two chains traverse overlapping regions of the log joint surface with no systematic drift, indicating that the burn-in of 100 samples is sufficient to reach approximate stationarity.  The between-chain separation visible in some draws is consistent with the known rotational non-identifiability of the latent embedding: chains may settle into distinct but equivalent rotation frames, which Procrustes alignment (Section~\ref{sec:inference}) resolves for all downstream summaries.

\subsection{Hilbert Space Gaussian Processes}
\label{appendix:HSGPs}
Traditionally, sampling from a full Gaussian Process is a computationally expensive procedure, involving inverting a typically large $N \times N$ covariance matrix $K$. \cite{Solin_2019} showed, however, that a reduced rank approximation of traditional stationary covariance matrices can be utilized instead, reducing computational burden. The resulting approximation represents the function $f(x)\approx \sum_{l=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l \psi_l(x)$ as a finite, weighted, linear combination of eigenvectors, $\psi_l(x)$, of the Laplacian on the domain of $x$, with the weights $\alpha_l \sim N(0,S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}))$, arising as a function of the spectral density $S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})$ evaluated at the associated eigenvalue $\sqrt{\lambda_l}$ of the stationary covariance matrix $K$. If $\mathcal{K}$ is the standard squared exponential kernel, $S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}) = s^2\sqrt{2\pi}\omega \text{exp}(\frac{-\omega^2\lambda_l}{2})$ is the spectral density of the squared exponential with length-scale parameter $\omega$ and variance parameter $s$. As we increase the number of dimensions, this approximation does not scale well, as eigenvalues for each d-tuple are needed. As such, this approach is useful for one-dimensional cases, where the domain $\Omega$ is bounded on  $[-L, L]$. 
In the one dimensional Dirichlet boundary condition case, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are $\lambda_j = (\frac{j \pi}{2L})^2$ and $\psi_l(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{L}}sin(\sqrt{\lambda_l}(L + x))$ respectively. 


For a full treatment of this approach, please refer to \cite{Solin_2019}.  


\subsection{Approximating a Separable Kernel}
\label{appendix:separability_properties}

We make the claim that
$g(x,t) \approx \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x) ( \sum_{l=1}^K \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t))$ has a separable covariance structure in $x$ and $t$. That is, $Cov( g(x,t) , g(x^{'},t^{'} ))$ can be factored into a product of kernels $K(t,t^{'})K(x,x^{'})$. 

First, we assume that $\alpha_l^i \sim N(0, S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}))$. Furthermore,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_separable}
Cov(\alpha_l^i, \alpha_z^k) = 
\begin{cases}
S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})& \text{if} \quad l = z, k = i \\
0 & \text{o.w}
\end{cases}
\end{align}
These assumptions align with the formulation outlined in \cite{Solin_2019}.

First, we will evaluate $Cov(\beta_i(t), \beta_i(t^{'}))$,
where $\beta_i(t) = \sum_{l=1}^K \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t)$.

\begin{align}
    Cov(\beta_i(t), \beta_i(t^{'})) = Cov(\sum_{l=1}^K \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t), \sum_{l=1}^K \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t^{'})) 
\end{align}
Using \eqref{eq:cov_separable}, we reach that 
\begin{align}
    Cov(\beta_i(t), \beta_i(t^{'})) = \sum_{l=1}^K  \psi_l(t^{'}) \psi_l(t) S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})
\end{align}
From \cite{Solin_2019}, $\sum_{l=1}^K  \psi_l(t^{'}) \psi_l(t) S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}) = \tilde{K}(t,t^{'})$ converges to $ K(t, t^{'})$ uniformly in the univariate case. Since we only consider the time dimension, this suffices for our purposes. 

Now, we can evaluate $Cov( g(x,t) , g(x^{'},t^{'} ))$.
\begin{align}
    Cov( g(x,t) , g(x^{'},t^{'} )) = Cov(\sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x)  \beta_i(t) , \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x^{'})  \beta_i(t^{'}) )
\end{align}
Note that from \eqref{eq:cov_separable},
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_separable_2}
Cov(\beta_i(t), \beta_j(t)) = 
\begin{cases}
\sum_{l=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}) \psi_l(t)^2& \text{if} \quad j = i \\
0 & \text{o.w}
\end{cases}
\end{align}
Thus,
\begin{align}
    Cov\!\left( g(x,t),\, g(x^{'},t^{'}) \right)
    &= \sum_{i=1}^M \phi(x)\phi(x^{'}) Cov\!\left(\beta_i(t), \beta_i(t^{'})\right) \nonumber\\
    &= \tilde{K}(t,t') \sum_{i=1}^M \phi(x)\phi(x^{'})
\end{align}
{\sloppy In our implementation, $\phi_i(x)$ is a linear (identity) projection, so $\sum_{i=1}^M \phi(x)\phi(x^{'}) = K_{\mathrm{lin}}(x,x^{'})$ exactly. The product $\tilde{K}(t,t^{'}) K_{\mathrm{lin}}(x,x^{'})$ therefore gives an exact separable covariance structure in the $x$ dimension, while the HSGP approximation $\tilde{K}(t,t^{'})$ converges uniformly to $K(t,t^{'})$ from \cite{Solin_2019}.\par}

\subsection{Enforcing Concavity}

\label{appendix:convexity}
\subsubsection{Validity of Prior}
\label{appendix:convexity_proof}
\begin{proof}
    
Let $\mu$ denote the law of $g(t)$ as a Gaussian measure on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$.  
The map
\[
T: g \mapsto \beta_0 + \beta_1(t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s g(z)^2 dz ds
\]
is continuous from $L^2([a,b])$ to $C([a,b])$ for any compact interval $[a,b]$, because:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Squaring is continuous from $L^2 \to L^1$
    \item Double integration is bounded from $L^1 \to C([a,b])$
\end{itemize}

By standard Gaussian measure theory, $\mu$ has full support in $L^2([a,b])$ under mild conditions on the covariance function.  
Therefore, for any $h_0$ smooth concave function on $[a,b]$, if we define
\[
g_0(t) = \sqrt{-h_0''(t)} \in L^2([a,b]),
\]
then for any $\epsilon>0$,
\[
\mu(\|g-g_0\|_{L^2}<\epsilon) > 0.
\]
By continuity of $T$, this implies
\[
\nu(\|h - h_0\|_\infty < \delta) > 0
\]
for some $\delta>0$ depending on $\epsilon$.  
Since smooth concave functions are dense in the set of continuous concave functions on $[a,b]$, the pushforward measure $\nu$ assigns positive mass to every uniform neighborhood of any continuous concave function.
The argument extends locally to all compact intervals, giving the result on $\mathbb{R}$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Univariate Case}
\label{appendix:univariate_concavity}
Considering we only wish our function $f(t)$ to be concave in the $t$ argument, we wish to find all set of $f(t)$ such that $\frac{\partial ^2 f(t)}{\partial t^2} \geq 0$. As such, our desired function has the form
\begin{align}
\label{eq:concave_uni_rep}
    f(t) = a + b(t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s \frac{\partial ^2 f(z)}{\partial z^2}dzds 
\end{align}
where $a, b$ are some constants of integration with respect to $t$. We show in the subsequent section how these constants of integration can be defined to modulate the shape of $f(t)$. 


Following the line of reasoning from \cite{convexityconstraints}, we assume that $\frac{\partial ^2 f(t)}{\partial t^2}$ can be modeled by $[g(t)]^2$, where $g(t) \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, K_t)$. 
In order to enforce convexity across the $t$ dimension, we use the Hilbert Space Gaussian Process (HSGP) approximation mentioned in \cite{convexityconstraints} to approximate $g(t) \approx \sum_{l=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l \psi_l(t)$.

Consequently,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:deriv_approx_uni_gp}
g(t)^2 \approx \sum_{l=1}^K \sum_{z=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_z})^{1/2} S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l \alpha_z \psi_l(t) \psi_z(t)\nonumber \\
 \approx  \sum_{l=1}^K \sum_{z=1}^K\tilde{\alpha_l} \tilde{\alpha_z} \psi_l(t)\psi_z(t) 
\end{align}

after letting $\tilde{\alpha}_l = S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l $.
As such, substituting \eqref{eq:deriv_approx_uni_gp} into \eqref{eq:concave_uni_rep} gives
\begin{align}
    & f(t) \approx a + b(t-t_0) - \sum_{l=1}^K \sum_{z=1}^K\tilde{\alpha_l} \tilde{\alpha_z}  \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s \psi_l(w)\psi_z(w)  dwds
\end{align}
 

Rewriting the above in terms of matrix products, and setting $t_0 = -L$,  yields
\begin{align}
\label{eq:final_gp_app_uni}
    f(t) \approx a + b(t+L) -  \mathbf{\tilde{\alpha}}^T \Psi(t) \mathbf{\tilde{\alpha}} 
\end{align} 


Here, $\mathbf{\tilde{\alpha}}$ is a $K$ vector with entries given by $\tilde{\alpha_l}$. 
The entries of the $K \times K$ matrix $\Psi(t)$ are given by
\begin{align}
\label{eq:phi_app}
\Psi_{lz}(t) =
\begin{cases} \frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})^2} +
\frac{(t+L)^2}{4L}  & \text{if } l = z \\
\frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})^2} - \frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{-}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{-})^2} & \text{if } l \neq z
\end{cases}
\end{align}

Here, $\epsilon_{lz}^{+} = \sqrt{\lambda_l} + \sqrt{\lambda_z}$, and $\epsilon_{lz}^{-} = \sqrt{\lambda_l} - \sqrt{\lambda_z}$. Note that since the eigen-functions $\psi_l(t)$ are sinusoidal, the integral in \eqref{eq:concave_uni_rep} can be computed in closed form. 


The concavity prior is placed on the latent mean $\mu_{ptk}$ in the linear predictor space; it does not, in general, guarantee concavity of the observable response after applying the inverse link.  However, the scientifically relevant properties are fully preserved.  All link functions used in the model --- exponential (Poisson, Negative-Binomial), logistic (Binomial, Beta-Binomial), and identity (Gaussian) --- are strictly monotone increasing.  For any strictly monotone $g^{-1}$,
\[
  \arg\max_t \; g^{-1}(\mu_{ptk}(t)) \;=\; \arg\max_t \; \mu_{ptk}(t),
\]
so the peak age $t^*_k$ is identical in the latent and observable spaces.  The peak value in the observable space, $g^{-1}(\mu_{ptk}(t^*_k))$, is a monotone function of the latent peak, so the rank ordering of peak values across players is also preserved.  The primary inferential targets of the model --- peak age, peak value, and the unimodal shape of the career arc --- are therefore exactly preserved under the link transformation, regardless of observation family.


\subsubsection{Properties of the Concave Prior}
The reparameterization in Theorem~\ref{theorem:concave_max_approx} has an important consequence for the prior's variance structure.  Writing the process explicitly as
\begin{equation}
  f(t) = f^* + (t - t^*)\!\int_{t_0}^{t^*} g(z)^2\,dz
         - \int_{t}^{t^*}\!\int_{t_0}^{s} g(z)^2\,dz\,ds,
  \label{eq:f_peak_rep}
\end{equation}
shows that at $t = t^*$ both integral terms vanish identically, so the GP component contributes zero variance at the peak; all uncertainty in $f(t^*)$ comes from the prior on $f^*$ alone.  Away from the peak the dominant contribution to $\operatorname{Var}(f(t) \mid t^*, f^*)$ is the squared double integral.  For a zero-mean GP $g$ with marginal variance $\sigma^2$, the Isserlis (Wick) theorem gives $E[g(z)^2 g(z')^2] = \sigma^4 + 2\mathcal{K}(z,z')^2$.  Integrating this over the triangular domain in \eqref{eq:f_peak_rep} yields
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(f(t) \mid t^*, f^*) \;\approx\; \tfrac{3}{4}\,\sigma^4\,(t - t^*)^4,
  \label{eq:var_quartic}
\end{equation}
so variance grows quartically with distance from the peak, scaling as $\sigma^4$ rather than $\sigma^2$ as in a standard GP.  This reflects the structural difficulty of the underlying inference problem: locating the peak of a function from $n$ temporally ordered observations is inherently harder than estimating its mean.  Mode estimation from $n$ observations converges at rate $n^{-2/5}$ under standard regularity conditions, compared with $n^{-1/2}$ for the mean; for NBA careers where $n$ is typically 5--12 seasons, this gap is substantial.  The $\sigma^4$ variance growth encodes this difficulty directly, placing greatest prior precision at the peak and allowing growing uncertainty with distance from it, consistent with the intuition that career trajectories are better identified near their apex than at the sparsely-observed early or late extremes.  Practically, this also means posterior uncertainty in $t^*$ propagates outward: a player whose peak age is uncertain will have wider credible bands across the full trajectory, not just at the peak.


\subsubsection{Multivariate Case}
\label{appendix:multivariate_concavity}
Considering we only wish our function $f(x,t)$ to be concave in the $t$ argument, but still incorporate information from another set of dimensions encoded by $x$, we wish to find all set of $f(x,t)$ such that $\frac{\partial ^2 f(x,t)}{\partial t^2} \geq 0$. As such, our desired function has the form
\begin{align}
\label{eq:concave_multi_rep_app}
    f(x,t) = a(x) + b(x)(t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s \frac{\partial ^2 f(x,z)}{\partial z^2}dzds
\end{align}
where $a(x), b(x)$ are some constants of integration with respect to $t$. We show in the subsequent section how these constants of integration can be defined to modulate the shape of $f(x,t)$.


Following the line of reasoning from \cite{convexityconstraints}, we assume that $\frac{\partial ^2 f(x,t)}{\partial t^2}$ can be modeled by $[g(x,t)]^2$, where $g(x,t) \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, K_x \otimes K_t)$. Note that this assumes that the time variable $t$ is separate from our $x$ variable . 

Using a linear projection $\phi_i(x)$ for the $x$ dimension (the identity map in our implementation), we can write $g(x,t) \approx \sum_{i=1}^M \beta_i(t) \phi_i(x)$. Here, $\beta_i(t)$ are $t$-dependent coefficients of the basis expansion. In order to enforce convexity across the $t$ dimension, we use the Hilbert Space Gaussian Process (HSGP) approximation mentioned in \cite{convexityconstraints} to approximate $\beta_i(t) \approx \sum_{l=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t)$. Note that this approximate representation preserves the separability of the covariance structure of our desired $g(x,t)$
\ref{appendix:separability_properties}.

If we let $\tilde{\alpha_l^i} = \alpha_l^i S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2}$  , then $g(x,t) \approx \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x) ( \sum_{l=1}^K \tilde{\alpha_l^i} \psi_l(t))$. 

Consequently,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:deriv_approx_gp}
g(x,t)^2 \approx \sum_{i=1}^M \sum_{j=1}^M \phi_j(x)\phi_i(x) \beta_j(t) \beta_i(t) \nonumber \\
\approx \sum_{i=1}^M \sum_{j=1}^M \phi_j(x)\phi_i(x) (\sum_{l=1}^K \tilde{\alpha_l^i} \psi_l(t)) (\sum_{l=1}^K \tilde{\alpha_l^j} \psi_l(t)) \nonumber \\  
 \approx \sum_{i=1}^M \sum_{j=1}^M \phi_j(x)\phi_i(x) (\sum_{l=1}^K \sum_{z=1}^K\tilde{\alpha_l^j} \tilde{\alpha_z^i} \psi_l(t)\psi_z(t) ) 
\end{align}

As such, substituting \eqref{eq:deriv_approx_gp} into \eqref{eq:concave_multi_rep} gives
\begin{align}
    & f(x,t) \approx a(x) + b(x)(t-t_0) -  \nonumber \\
    & \sum_{i=1}^M \sum_{j=1}^M \phi_j(x)\phi_i(x) (\sum_{l=1}^K \sum_{z=1}^K\tilde{\alpha_l^j} \tilde{\alpha_z^i}  \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s \psi_l(w)\psi_z(w)  dwds)
\end{align}
 

Rewriting the above in terms of matrix products, and setting $t_0 = -L$,  yields
\begin{align}
\label{eq:final_gp_app}
    f(x,t) \approx a(x) + b(x)(t+L) -  \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T\mathbf{\tilde{A}}^T \Psi(t) \mathbf{\tilde{A}} \mathbf{\phi}(x)
\end{align} 


Here, $\mathbf{\tilde{A}}$ is a $M \times K$ matrix with entries given by $\tilde{\alpha_l^j}$. 
The entries of the $K \times K$ matrix $\Psi(t)$ are given by \ref{eq:phi} from the univariate case. 




\subsubsection{Controlling the Peak Behavior}
\label{appendix:peak_value}
We may wish to place a prior on the location of the peak of our curve, as well as the value that this peak takes. Thus, our formulation begins with the following constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $f(x,t_{max}) = f_{max}$
    \item $\frac{d}{dt} f(x,t) |_{t = t_{max}} = 0$
\end{enumerate}

The first expectation illustrates that at our peak $t_{max}$, our function takes on the value $c$. The second expectation requires that our first derivative with respect to $t$ is 0 at $t_{max}$. Since our function is concave by construction, we do not need to constrain the behavior of the second derivative. Note here that $t_{max}$ and $f_{max}$ are specified apriori.


Taking the partial derivative of \eqref{eq:final_gp} with respect to $t$, and setting it equal to 0 yields the following requirement
\begin{align}
\label{eq:first_deriv_constraint}
    b(x) = \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T\mathbf{\tilde{A}}^T \Psi^{'}(t_{max}) \mathbf{\tilde{A}} \mathbf{\phi}(x)
\end{align}
Here, $\Psi^{'}$ is the first derivative of the entries of \eqref{eq:phi} with respect to $t$. 
Now, it remains to satisfy the peak value constraint. Formally,

\begin{align}
\label{eq:peak_value_constraint}
    a(x) = f_{max} - b(x)(t_{max} - t_0) + \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T \mathbf{\tilde{A}}^T \Psi(t_{max}) \mathbf{\tilde{A}} \mathbf{\phi}(x)
\end{align}
If we combine \eqref{eq:first_deriv_constraint} and \eqref{eq:peak_value_constraint}, and plug the resulting values for $D(x)$ and $C(x)$ into \eqref{eq:final_gp}, we are met with the following theorem.

\begin{align}
\label{eq:max_constraint_gp_app}
    f(x,t) \approx f_{max}  +  \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T\mathbf{\tilde{A}}^T [ \Psi(t_{max}) - \Psi(t) + \Psi^{'}(t_{max})(t - t_{max}) ] \mathbf{\tilde{A}} \mathbf{\phi}(x)
\end{align}
Note that when $t = t_{max}$, we recover $f_{max}$ as desired.

Subsequently, letting $f_{max}(x) , t_{max}(x) \sim GP(0, K_x)$ allows us to pool information across the $x$ dimension, allowing similar players to have similar peaks / peak-values.

\subsubsection{Convergence of the Push-Forward HSGP Approximation}


\begin{proposition}[Convergence of the HSGP approximation]
\label{prop:hsgp_convergence_proof}
Let $k$ be a stationary covariance function on $[0,T]$ with spectral
density $S$, and let $\{\phi_i,\lambda_i\}_{i=1}^\infty$ be the Laplacian
eigenpairs on $[0,T]$.
Define $\omega_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$.
Let
\[
g(t)
=
\sum_{i=1}^\infty
\sqrt{S(\omega_i)}\,\alpha_i \psi_i(t),
\qquad
\alpha_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1),
\]
and its truncation
\[
g_K(t)
=
\sum_{i=1}^K
\sqrt{S(\omega_i)}\,\alpha_i \psi_i(t).
\]
Define
\[
f(t)
=
\int_0^t \int_0^s g(z)^2\,dz\,ds,
\qquad
f_K(t)
=
\int_0^t \int_0^s g_K(z)^2\,dz\,ds.
\]
Then
\[
f_K \to f
\quad \text{uniformly on } [0,T] \text{ almost surely}.
\]
\end{proposition}


\begin{proof}
By the HSGP construction (\cite{Solin_2019}), the covariance
operator admits the spectral approximation
\[
k(t,t')
\approx
\sum_{i=1}^K S(\omega_i)\psi_i(t)\psi_i(t').
\]
Under $\sum_i S(\omega_i)<\infty$, the Gaussian series defining $g$
converges almost surely in $L^2([0,T])$, and
\[
\mathbb{E}\|g-g_K\|_{L^2}^2
=
\sum_{i=K+1}^\infty S(\omega_i)
\to 0.
\]
Hence $g_K \to g$ almost surely in $L^2$.
Using
\[
\|g_K^2 - g^2\|_{L^1}
\le
\|g_K-g\|_{L^2}\|g_K+g\|_{L^2},
\]
we obtain $g_K^2 \to g^2$ in $L^1$ almost surely.
The double-integration operator
\[
I(h)(t)=\int_0^t\int_0^s h(z)\,dz\,ds
\]
is bounded from $L^1([0,T])$ to $C([0,T])$.
Therefore $f_K = I(g_K^2) \to I(g^2)=f$ uniformly almost surely.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Convexity Formulation Properties}
\label{appendix:convexity_properties}
\subsubsection{Separability}
We wish to understand how the convexity constraints affect the overall separability of our process. We will assume the formulation presented in \ref{sec:proposed_model}, where the maximum location $t_{max}$ and maximum value $f_{max}$ are specified. 
{\sloppy We also assume that $g(x,t) \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, K_x \otimes K_t)$, so that $Cov(g(x,t), g(x^{'},t^{'})) = K(x,x^{'})K(t,t^{'})$, and that $g(x,t)$ has marginal variance $\sigma^2$.\par}

Initially, we first aim to ascertain $Cov(g(x,t)^2,g(x^{'},t^{'})^2)$.
We can rewrite the covariance as the following through the definition of covariance:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:cov_1}
    &Cov\!\left(g(x,t)^2,\,g(x^{'},t^{'})^2\right) \nonumber\\
    &\quad = E[g(x,t)g(x,t)g(x^{'},t^{'})g(x^{'},t^{'})] - E[g(x^{'},t^{'})^2]\,E[g(x,t)^2]
\end{align}


We can rewrite the first expectation using a special case of \cite{isserlis} Theorem as the following:

\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_2}
E[g(x,t)g(x,t)g(x^{'},t^{'})g(x^{'},t^{'})] = \nonumber \\ E[g(x^{'},t^{'})^2]E[g(x,t)^2] + 2E[g(x,t)g(x^{'},t^{'})]^2 
\end{align}

Inserting \eqref{eq:cov_2} into \eqref{eq:cov_1}, we see that 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_3}
    Cov(g(x,t)^2,g(x^{'},t^{'})^2) = \nonumber \\ 2E[g(x,t)g(x^{'},t^{'})]^2 = \nonumber\\ 2Cov(g(x,t), g(x^{'},t^{'}))^2 = \nonumber\\ 2K(x,x^{'})^2K(t,t^{'})^2 
\end{align}. 

Note that this term is separable. 
It is now important to evaluate
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_integral}
&Cov \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{\max(t^{'},k(x^{'}))} h(z^{'}; t^{'}, k(x^{'})) g(x^{'},z^{'})^2 dz^{'}, \nonumber\\
&\qquad \int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k(x))} h(z; t, k(x)) g(x,z)^2 dz \bigg)
\end{align}.

Given that we are working with Gaussian Processes, $g(x,t)$ is sufficiently smooth and regular in the $t$ dimension such that we are able to interchange the Covariance operation and the integral. As such, we can rewrite $\eqref{eq:cov_integral}$ using Fubini's Theorem, and making a key substitution from the result in \eqref{eq:cov_3} yields that 

{\small
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_integral_interchange}
&Cov \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{\max(t^{'},k(x^{'}))} h\, g(x^{'},z^{'})^2 dz^{'}, \int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k(x))} h\, g(x,z)^2 dz \bigg) = \nonumber \\
&\iint h(z^{'}; t^{'}, k(x^{'})) h(z; t, k(x))\, E[g(x,z)^2 g(x^{'},z^{'})^2] \, dz \, dz^{'} \nonumber \\
&\quad - \int h(z; t, k(x)) E[g(x,z)^2]\,dz
    \cdot \int h(z^{'}; t^{'}, k(x^{'})) E[g(x^{'},z)^2]\,dz \nonumber \\
&= 2K(x,x^{'})^2 \iint h(z; t, k(x))\, h(z^{'}; t^{'}, k(x^{'}))\, K(z,z^{'})^2 \, dz \, dz^{'} \nonumber
\end{align}
}

Note however that the bounds of the integral are dependent on a function of both $t, x$. As a result, the covariance of this process is not separable.


\subsubsection{Expectation}
We will assume the formulation presented in \ref{sec:proposed_model}, where the maximum location $t_{max}$ and maximum value $f_{max}$ are specified. 
Thus,
\begin{align}
    E[f(x,t) | t_{max}(x), f_{max}(x)] & = f_{max}(x) +  \\ & \cdot(t-t_{max}(x))\int_{t_0}^{t_{max}(x)}E[g(x,z)^2 | x,z]dz  \nonumber \\ & -\int_{t}^{t_{max}(x)}  \int_{t_0}^s E[g(x,z)^2 | x,z]dzds \nonumber
\end{align}
Noting that our assumption was that $g(x,t)$ is a zero-mean GP with separable covariance in $x$ and $t$, $E[g(x,z)^2|x,z] = Var[g(x,z)|x,z] = \sigma^2_x\sigma^2_t = \sigma^2$. 
Thus, 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:expectation}
    E[f(x,t)|t_{max}(x), f_{max}(x)] & = f_{max}(x) + \sigma^2(t_{max}(x) - t_0)\cdot(t-t_{max}(x)) \nonumber \\ & -\sigma^2 \frac{(t_{max}(x)-t)^2}{2} 
\end{align}

If given $x$, $t_{max}(x) \sim Unif[t_0 = -L, L]$, we can compute the full expectation from \eqref{eq:expectation}.

\begin{align}
    E[f(x,t)] = f_{max}(x)  - \frac{\sigma^2 L^2}{2} + \sigma^2 Lt -  \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2} = f_{max}(x) - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}(t - L)^2
\end{align}
\subsubsection{Variance}
The law of iterated variance suggests that computing the full variance of our process involves computing $Var[f(x,t) | t_{max}, f_{max}]$, and $E[f(x,t) | t_{max}, f_{max}]$. We have already computed the latter in the previous section. 

\begin{align}
    \label{eq:variance_process}
    Var[f(x,t) | t_{max}(x), f_{max}(x)] & = \nonumber \\ & Var\bigg[ (t - t_{max}(x)) \cdot \int_{t_0}^{t_{max}(x)} g(x,z)^2 dz - \nonumber \\ & \int_{t}^{t_{max}(x)} \int_{t_0}^s g(x,z)^2 dzds \bigg] 
\end{align}.

Now, it remains to simplify the variance in \eqref{eq:variance_process}.
For notational brevity, let $t_{max}(x) = k$. We will start by first simplifying the integral sum, and by assuming that $t \leq k$.
\begin{align}
    \int_{t_0}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz - \int_t^k \int_{t_0}^s g(x,z)^2 dz ds \nonumber = \\
    \int_{t_0}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz - \int_{t_0}^k \int_{max(t,z)}^k g(x,z)^2dsdz = \nonumber \\
    \int_{t_0}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_{t_0}^t (t - k) g(x,z)^2dz - \int_{t}^k (k -z) g(x,z)^2dz  = \nonumber \\
     2 \int_{t_0}^t (t - k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_{t}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_{t}^k (k -z) g(x,z)^2dz = \nonumber \\
      2 \int_{t_0}^t (t - k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_{t}^k (t-z) g(x,z)^2dz
\end{align}

Now, if $t \geq k$,

\begin{align}
    \int_{t_0}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz - \int_t^k \int_{t_0}^s g(x,z)^2 dz ds \nonumber = \\
    2 \int_{t_0}^k (t - k) g(x,z)^2dz + 
 \int_{k}^t (z-t) g(x,z)^2dz
\end{align}

Thus, combining both cases,

\begin{align}
\label{eq:final_integral}
    \int_{t_0}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_t^k \int_{t_0}^s g(x,z)^2 dz ds \nonumber = \\
    2 \int_{t_0}^{min(t,k)} (t - k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_{min(k,t)}^{max(t,k)} (t-z) g(x,z)^2dz
\end{align}

Let's define the following to simplify our expression

\begin{align}
h(z; t, k) = 
    \begin{cases}
        2(t-k) \quad z \in [t_0, min(t,k)] \\
        (t-z) \quad z \in [min(t,k), max(t,k)] \\
        0 \quad o.w
    \end{cases}
\end{align}

Thus,
\begin{align}
    Var[f(x,t) | t_{max}(x), f_{max}(x)] & = Var[\int_{t_0}^{max(t,k)} h(z; t, k) g(x,z)^2 dz]
\end{align}


Now, in order to compute the variance of this term, we will resort to the definition.

\begin{align}
    Var[\int_{t_0}^{max(t,k)} h(z; t, k) g(x,z)^2 dz] = E[(\int_{t_0}^{max(t,k)} h(z; t, k) g(x,z)^2 dz)^2] - \nonumber\\ E[\int_{t_0}^{max(t,k)} h(z; t, k) g(x,z)^2 dz]^2
\end{align}

Again, for notational brevity, let $ b = max(k,t)$. 

We will start by computing the expectation.

\begin{align}
    E[\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t,k) g(x,z)^2dz] = \nonumber \\
    \int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k) E[g(x,z)^2]dz = \nonumber \\
    \sigma^2 \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k)dz \bigg)^2
\end{align}

Next, we will compute the second moment.

\begin{align}
    E[\bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t,k) g(x,z)^2dz\bigg)^2] = \nonumber \\
    E[\bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b}\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t,k)h(z^{'}; t, k)g(x,z^{'})^2 g(x,z)^2dzdz^{'}\bigg)] = \nonumber \\
    \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b}\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k)h(z^{'}; t, k) E[g(x,z^{'})^2 g(x,z)^2] dzdz^{'}\bigg) = \nonumber \\
    \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b}\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k)h(z^{'}; t, k) (\sigma^4 + 2 K(z,z^{'})^2) dzdz^{'}\bigg) = \nonumber \\
    [\sigma^2 \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k)dz \bigg)]^2 + 2\bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b}\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k)h(z^{'}; t, k) K(z,z^{'})^2dzdz^{'}\bigg)
\end{align}

Thus, the total variance is 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:conditional_variance}
    &Var\!\left[\int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t, k) g(x,z)^2\,dz\right] = \nonumber\\
    &\quad 2\!\left(\int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k)}\!\int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t, k)\, h(z^{'}; t, k)\,K(z,z^{'})^2\,dz\,dz^{'}\right)
\end{align}

This is quite difficult evaluate since the integrand involves the square of a kernel, however we can bound this variance by noting that $K(z,z^{'}) \leq \sigma^2$. 

Re-evaluating \eqref{eq:conditional_variance}, 

\begin{align}
     Var\!\left[\int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t,k) g(x,z)^2\,dz\right] \leq 2 \sigma^4 \left[\int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t, k)\, dz\right]^2
\end{align}

Now, lets make the same assumption we made in the previous section, $k \sim Unif[t_0 = -L, L]$. 

We can now take the expectation of this conditional variance over $k$.

\begin{align}
    E_k[Var(Y(k))] &\leq 2\sigma^4\, E_k\!\left[\left(\int_{-L}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t, k)\, dz\right)^2\right] \nonumber\\
    &= \frac{1}{2L} \int_{-L}^L\left[\int_{-L}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t, k)\, dz\right]^2 dk
\end{align}

Note that we have a random variable $k$, which renders the integral a random variable. 

Unfortunately, after much mathematics, we are left with

\begin{align}
\label{eq:expectation_k}
    E_k[Var(Y(k))] \leq \frac{\sigma^4}{L} \bigg[\int_{-L}^t [2(t-k)(k+L) +  \frac{(k-t)^2}{2}]^2 dk + \nonumber \\ \int_t^L [2(t-k)(t+L) - \frac{(k-t)^2}{2}]^2 dk \bigg] 
\end{align}

It remains to compute $Var_k[E[f(x,t) | f_{max}, k]$. 

Recall from \eqref{eq:expectation},

\begin{align}
\label{eq:expectation_simple}
     E[f(x,t)|k, f_{max}(x)] & = f_{max}(x) + \sigma^2(k + L)\cdot(t-k) \nonumber \\ & -\sigma^2 \frac{(k-t)^2}{2} 
\end{align}.

Through very tedious computation,

\begin{align}
\label{eq:variance_k}
    Var_k[ E[f(x,t)|k, f_{max}(x)]] = \sigma^4 \Bigg[
&\frac{9L^4}{20}
+ (-tL + t^2)L^2 \nonumber \\
&+ \frac{L^2}{3}\left(4t^2 - 4tL + L^2\right) \nonumber \\
&+ \left(tL - \frac{t^2}{2} \right)^2
- \left(tL - \frac{L^2}{2} - \frac{t^2}{2} \right)^2
\Bigg] 
\end{align}

Finally, we can bound the variance of our process as the sum of \eqref{eq:variance_k} and \eqref{eq:expectation_k} plus some constants regarding the expectation and variance of $f_{max}$, noting that $f_{max} ,t_{max}$ are independent.
