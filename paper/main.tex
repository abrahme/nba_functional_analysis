\section{Introduction}

Understanding how NBA player performance evolves with age is central to both roster construction and player development.  General managers must project whether a player’s production will hold over the life of a proposed contract --- the Brooklyn Nets’ 2013 trade for aging stars Paul Pierce and Kevin Garnett (35 and 37 years old), exchanging future draft picks and young players, illustrates the franchise-altering cost of misjudging career trajectories.  From the athlete’s perspective, knowing which skills decline earliest can guide training adjustments: guards may shift toward shooting and decision-making, while bigs may emphasize positioning over athleticism as their careers progress.  \textit{Production curve analysis} formalizes this challenge as inferring smooth trajectories $f_p(t)$ from sparse longitudinal observations $Y_{pt} \approx f_p(t) + \varepsilon_p(t)$ across players \citep{wakim2014functional}.  A principled model must: (i) handle multiple performance metrics jointly, (ii) enforce the well-documented concavity of athletic careers \citep[see e.g.][]{vaci2019large}, (iii) respect the sampling distributions of count and proportion data, and (iv) learn player similarity from the data rather than from pre-specified positional labels.

Existing methods satisfy at most two of these desiderata.  The bulk of the production curve literature models individual metrics in isolation \citep{page2007using, vaci2019large, wakim2014functional}, ignoring the rich joint structure across shooting, rebounding, and composite performance measures.  Rating systems such as RAPTOR \citep[Robust Algorithm using Player Tracking and On/Off Ratings;][]{natesilver538_2015, natesilver538_2019} estimate a single summary score via nearest-neighbor averaging of similar historical players, but impose similarity through fixed feature comparisons and are susceptible to selection bias at career extremes.  \citet{vaci2019large} improve upon naive averages by fitting piecewise parametric curves with the peak location specified a priori, but the rigid functional form limits adaptability to diverse career shapes.  Methods that do incorporate player pooling either use categorical positional types \citep{page2007using} or pre-specified continuous similarity \citep{wakim2014functional, vinue2019forecasting}; in particular, \citet{vinue2019forecasting} represent each player as a convex combination of a fixed set of archetypoids \citep{vinue2015archetypoids}, constraining the representation to the convex hull of an a-priori determined archetype set.  No existing approach jointly models multiple metrics as concave-constrained functions while simultaneously learning player similarity from the data.

We address this gap with a Bayesian multi-task Gaussian Process Latent Variable Model (GPLVM) that jointly estimates concave production curves across 17 performance metrics and $\approx$\,2300 NBA players (1997--2021).  Each player is assigned a latent embedding inferred from the full metric profile; players with similar embeddings learn similar aging trajectories without relying on positional labels or hand-specified archetypes.  Concavity is enforced through a novel nonparametric prior (the Concave Process) derived analytically from an HSGP approximation.  A Weibull survival model accounts for right-censored careers, correcting the survivor bias that afflicts conditioning on observed seasons alone.  Posterior analysis of the learned embedding then supports (a) latent-space nearest-neighbor retrieval of career-comparable players, (b) hierarchical archetype discovery, and (c) counterfactual career estimation for injury-affected players.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.  Section~\ref{sec:proposed_model} introduces the observation model, concave process prior, and the joint generative model.  Section~\ref{sec:inference} describes MAP-initialized MCMC and Procrustes alignment for posterior summary.  Section~\ref{sec:results} presents posterior curves, model validation, latent space analysis, and injury counterfactuals.  Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

Our primary methodological contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Concave Process prior.}  We derive a novel nonparametric prior over concave functions by applying a continuous push-forward map to an HSGP-approximated Gaussian process.  The resulting prior assigns positive mass to every uniform neighborhood of any continuous concave function, and admits an analytic closed form that makes it tractable for large-scale Bayesian inference.

    \item \textbf{Joint multi-metric GPLVM with concavity constraints.}  We embed each player in a shared low-dimensional latent space and use this embedding to parameterize a family of metric-specific concave aging curves simultaneously.  By respecting the native sampling distributions of count and proportion data (Binomial, Poisson, Negative-Binomial, Beta, Gaussian), the model avoids the Gaussianity assumptions that distort inference for low-minute players.

    \item \textbf{Survival-adjusted inference.}  An explicit Weibull career-duration model accounts for right-censored and left-truncated careers, correcting the survivor bias that arises when conditioning only on observed seasons.

    \item \textbf{Covariate-anchored latent variable prior.}  Standard GPLVMs place an uninformative prior $X_p \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_q)$ on the latent coordinates, yielding a posterior identified only up to orthogonal rotation.  We instead place a structured prior $X_p \sim \mathcal{N}(Z_p^\top W,\, \sigma_X^2\, I_q)$ that anchors each player's latent position toward a learned projection of exogenous covariates (draft standing, height, and positional role).  Because $W$ and $\sigma_X$ are inferred jointly with the rest of the model, it learns both which combination of physical and scouting signals best predicts latent position and how tightly that prior should constrain the embedding.  This provides a model-level resolution to rotational non-identifiability that complements the posterior Procrustes alignment of Section~\ref{sec:inference}, and renders the learned embedding interpretable in terms of observable player characteristics.

\end{enumerate}

Building on the learned posterior embedding, we demonstrate three analytical payoffs: (a) \textbf{latent-space nearest-neighbor retrieval} (identifying career-comparable players using posterior uncertainty over the embedding); (b) \textbf{hierarchical archetype discovery} (unsupervised clustering of aging profiles without positional labels, with per-metric peak-age and peak-value characterization); and (c) \textbf{injury counterfactual estimation} (projecting the career arc a player would have followed absent a career-altering injury such as ACL or Achilles rupture, enabling more principled analysis of injury impact).




\section{Data and Exploratory Analysis}


In this work, we use data from \url{basketball-reference.com} \citep{bballref} scraped using the package \texttt{ballr} \citep{ballr}. Our data consists of $\approx$ 2300 NBA players from years 1997 - 2021, from the ages of 18 - 39. The data can be described as a longitudinal mixed tensor $\mathcal{Y}$ of size $P$ by $T$ by $K$ where $P$ is the number of players, $T$ is the range of ages observed across players' careers, and $K$ is the number of production metric curves; $\mathcal{Y}_{ptk}$ is missing if player $p$ is not observed for metric $k$ at age $t$.  $\Omega$ is a binary tensor of the same dimensionality as $\mathcal{Y}$ indicating missing entries.  Players may have unobserved values due to injury, retirement, and left/right-censoring. The full list of metrics is given in Table \ref{tab:metric_description}. Metric definitions follow \cite{terner2020modeling}.

In order to analyze such a complex data set, it is important to take advantage of the structure in the data. There are primarily three sources of variation, which intuitively align with each of the modes of the data tensor. However, within each mode, there exist inherent correlation which arises from how the sport is played, and has developed.


First, there exists an inherent low-rank structure to player similarity, from a domain-knowledge perspective. Players tend to fall into one of three categories (Guards, Forwards, and Centers) which can account for variation in shooting tendencies (Guards tend to be able to shoot better and from further out), as well as defensive metrics (Centers and Forwards typically accrue more blocks, Guards with more steals). Although these positional categories are not quite accurate, they do suggest some sort of low dimensional structure to the player space \cite{chessa2023complex}.

In addition to a low rank structure among the players, there also exists some low rank structure in the metric space. Shooting metrics tend to be correlated with one another as well, and players who are good at offensive rebounding will also likely be good at rebounding on the defensive end (Figure~\ref{fig:metric_corr} in the Appendix).

Finally, there is also some substructure to how metrics evolve with time. According to the literature of skill acquisition and aging, certain skills tend to rise in proficiency over time, and stabilize, resulting in a later peak. \section{Proposed Model}

\label{sec:proposed_model}


% In line with the existing literature on production curve analysis, we assume that each observation $\mathcal{Y}_{ptk}$ at the player $p$, time $t$, and metric $k$ level is sampled from a latent mean $\mu_{ptk}$, and transformed by the appropriate link for the sampling distribution 


\subsection{Likelihood}
Let $y_{ptk}$ be the measured outcome for player $p$ at age $t$, in metric $k$.  We model data at a yearly resolution between the ages of 18 and 40 ($t \in \{18, ..., 40\})$ over the 17 metrics shown in Figure \ref{fig:empirical_production_players} ($k \in \{1, ..., 17\})$. We consider five classes of observation models: Gaussian, Binomial, Poisson, Negative-Binomial, and Beta. Metrics which are inherently fractions (e.g.\ field goal percentage) are assumed to be conditionally Binomially distributed,

\begin{equation}
y_{ptk} \sim \text{Binomial}(n^k_{ptk}, \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\mu_{ptk}))  \text{ if } \text{k} \in \mathcal{B}
\end{equation}
%
where $n_{ptk}$ is the number of trials, $\mu_{ptk}$ is the logit success probability, and $\mathcal{B}$ is the set of indices corresponding to made-shot metrics.  Raw volume counts (BLK, STL, AST, DREB, OREB, TOV) are modeled as conditionally Poisson offset by minutes played:
\begin{equation}
y_{ptk} \sim \text{Poisson}(m_{pt} \cdot \exp(\mu_{ptk}))  \text{ if } \text{k} \in \mathcal{C}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of Poisson count metrics. Attempt totals (FTA, FG2A, FG3A), which exhibit overdispersion beyond Poisson, are modeled as Negative-Binomial:
\begin{equation}
y_{ptk} \sim \text{Negative-Binomial}(m_{pt} \cdot \exp(\mu_{ptk}), \tau^2_k)  \text{ if } \text{k} \in \mathcal{R}
\end{equation}
where $\tau^2_k$ is the overdispersion parameter and $\mathcal{R}$ is the set of Negative-Binomial attempt metrics. For continuous valued metrics (composite metrics such as OBPM and DBPM), we assume a conditionally Gaussian model:
\begin{equation}
y_{ptk} \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_{ptk}, \sigma^2 / m_{pt})  \text{ if } \text{k} \in \mathcal{G}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{G}$ corresponds to the indices of real-valued metrics. Proportion metrics bounded in $(0,1)$ — specifically usage rate (USG) and percentage of available minutes played (PCT Minutes) — are modeled as conditionally Beta distributed:
\begin{equation}
y_{ptk} \sim \text{Beta}(\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\mu_{ptk}),\, \tau^2_k)  \text{ if } \text{k} \in \mathcal{P}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}$ is the set of indices corresponding to proportion metrics and we use the mean-variance parameterization.

Finally, we consider two special cases for playing-time.  Let $g_{pt}$ denote the total number of games played and $m_{pt}$ denote the minutes per game played by player $p$ at time $t$.  We assume that
\begin{align}
g_{pt} &\sim \text{Beta-Bin}(82, \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\mu^g_{pt}), \tau_1^2)\\
m_{pt} &\sim 48\cdot \text{Beta}(\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\mu^m_{pt}), \tau_2^2 /g_{pt})
\end{align}
that is, games played follows a Beta-Binomial distribution with 82 trials (the maximum games in a season) and minutes-per-game follows a scaled Beta distribution (scaled by 48, the maximum minutes in a game).  In both of the above, we use the mean-variance parameterization.

\subsection{Mean Model}

The core challenge is how to specify $f^k(X_p,t)$ in a way that shares information across players $p$, time $t$, and metrics $k$ simultaneously.  In order to capture similarity across players, we introduce a $q$-dimensional latent variable $X_p \in \mathbb{R}^q$ for each player $p$, which we treat as an unknown and infer from the data. This induces dependence in the average performance across all $K$ metrics and sets up a natural borrowing of information: players with similar latent coordinates learn similar aging curve shapes, peak ages, and peak values.

We encode physical domain knowledge directly in the prior on $X_p$.  Let $Z_p \in \mathbb{R}^4$ collect standardized player-level covariates --- negative log draft position, height, and two positional group indicators (Forward and Guard, with Center as the baseline) --- and let $W \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times q}$ be a learned projection matrix with hierarchical scale $\sigma_W$.  We place the structured prior
\begin{equation}
  X_p \sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(Z_p^\top W,\; \sigma_X^2\, I_q\bigr),
  \label{eq:structured_prior}
\end{equation}
which pulls each player's latent position toward the subspace predicted by draft standing, physical size, and positional role, while allowing the data to override that prior mean at individual scale $\sigma_X$.  When $\sigma_W \to 0$ this reduces to the isotropic $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_X^2 I_q)$.  The full specification and identifiability discussion appear in Section~\ref{sec:player_similarity}.

We model the natural parameter $\mu_{ptk}$ as the sum of a concave function $f^k(X_p,t)$, which captures aging-related behavior informed by the player embedding $X_p$, and $r^k_p(t) \sim \mathrm{AR}(1)$, which captures idiosyncratic deviations from the underlying aging trend for each player and metric:

\begin{equation}
\mu_{ptk} = f^k(X_p,t) + r^k_p(t)
\end{equation}
%


Note that $\mu_{ptk}$ reflects the ``true'' ability of the player in the given metric at a given time.  Here, $f^k(X_p,t)$ captures aspects of performance that are predictable, given the performance of similar players over time, whereas $r_p^k(t)$ reflects aspects of the player's true skill that are not predictable (e.g.\ due to injury, change of strategy or role).  These components are distinct from the sampling variability due to finite observations, which arise in the likelihoods outlined in the previous section.

The AR(1) residual $r^k_p(t)$ is motivated by two empirical regularities.  First, a player who exceeds their archetype-predicted output in a given season --- due to a new team system, an expanded role, or a mid-career skill acquisition --- tends to sustain that elevated production for at least one to two additional seasons, producing positive serial correlation in the residuals beyond what the concave mean captures.  Conversely, a player in a temporary slump tends to underperform for multiple consecutive seasons before reverting.  Second, the concave GP mean is shared across all players with similar latent embeddings and therefore cannot capture the idiosyncratic year-to-year fluctuations peculiar to an individual player-metric combination; a parsimonious one-parameter first-order autoregression absorbs this individual-level momentum efficiently.  The empirical benefit is confirmed in Section~\ref{sec:validation}: the Concave$+$AR model achieves significantly better ELPPD than both the AR-only baseline and the Concave-only model on the counting statistics (BLK, STL, DREB, OREB) where individual form varies most substantially season to season.

The embedding $X_p$ enters $f^k$ through a projection $\phi(X_p)$ that determines each player's metric-specific peak age and peak value; any suitable kernel map of $X_p$ yields a valid construction.  The player-specific aging curves are then concave functions whose shape parameters are bilinear functions of $\phi(X_p)$, so that players with similar embeddings learn similar trajectories.  The full construction is given in Sections~\ref{sec:concave_prior} and~\ref{sec:player_similarity}.




\subsection{Career Duration Model}
\label{sec:survival}

Not every player is observed for the full span of seasons covered by the data.  Some retire voluntarily, others are forced out by injury, and the remainder are still active when the data are collected.  Ignoring this selection would bias estimates of the aging curve: players who exit early are systematically different from those who persist.  We therefore augment the performance likelihood with an explicit model for career duration that accounts for right-censored observations.

\subsubsection{Weibull Proportional-Hazard Formulation}

Let $T_p$ denote the \emph{exit season} of player $p$ and let $\alpha_p$ denote their \emph{entrance season}.  We model the residual time at risk $T_p \mid T_p > \alpha_p$ with a player-specific Weibull distribution
\begin{equation}
    T_p \mid \alpha_p \;\sim\; \operatorname{Weibull}(\eta_p,\,\gamma_p),
    \label{eq:weibull}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_p > 0$ is the \emph{scale} (expected career length, in seasons) and $\gamma_p > 0$ is the \emph{concentration} (shape).  A concentration greater than one encodes an increasing hazard—players become progressively more likely to exit as they age, consistent with age-related attrition.

\paragraph{Parameter maps.}  Both parameters are linked to the latent embedding $X_p \in \mathbb{R}^q$ via a bilinear projection shared with the performance likelihood:
\begin{align}
    \tilde{\eta}_p   &= \frac{\sigma_{\text{exit}}}{\sqrt{q}}\,X_p^\top \psi_{\text{exit}}, \qquad
    \eta_p = \exp\!\bigl(\mu_{\text{scale}} + \tilde{\eta}_p\bigr), \label{eq:eta} \\
    \tilde{\gamma}_p &= X_p^\top \psi_{\text{rate}} + \delta_0,\qquad
    \gamma_p = 1 + 2\,\sigma(\tilde{\gamma}_p), \label{eq:gamma}
\end{align}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function, $\mu_{\text{scale}} \sim \mathcal{N}(\log 11.5,\, 0.5)$ is a global log-scale offset, $\sigma_{\text{exit}} \sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(0.5)$ controls the player-specific spread in career length, and $\delta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\log 10,\, 0.5)$ is a global offset on the log-odds of the concentration.  The log-linear scale places the prior central career length near 11.5 seasons while allowing the latent embedding to shift $\eta_p$ multiplicatively; the sigmoid on $\tilde{\gamma}_p$ constrains $\gamma_p \in (1, 3)$, encoding the prior belief that career-exit hazard is increasing.  The latent embedding thereby links career longevity to the same low-dimensional player representation that drives performance.

\subsubsection{Truncated Likelihood and Censoring}

Because player $p$ is only observed after season $\alpha_p$, the exit-time likelihood must be left-truncated at $\alpha_p$.  For a Weibull with cumulative hazard
\begin{equation}
    H(t;\,\eta_p,\gamma_p) = \left(\frac{t}{\eta_p}\right)^{\gamma_p},
\end{equation}
the truncated log-likelihood contributions are:
\begin{equation}
    \ell_p =
    \begin{cases}
        \log h(T_p;\,\eta_p,\gamma_p) - \bigl[H(T_p;\,\eta_p,\gamma_p) - H(\alpha_p;\,\eta_p,\gamma_p)\bigr] & \text{(observed exit)}, \\
        -\bigl[H(T_p;\,\eta_p,\gamma_p) - H(\alpha_p;\,\eta_p,\gamma_p)\bigr] & \text{(right-censored)},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:surv_lik}
\end{equation}
where $h(t;\,\eta,\gamma) = (\gamma/\eta)(\,t/\eta)^{\gamma-1}$ is the Weibull hazard.

Joint inference over the performance and survival components proceeds by summing the log-densities from both models, so that the latent embedding $X_p$ is simultaneously informed by what a player \emph{did} on the court and how long they \emph{stayed} in the league.

Evidence across multiple sports shows that production rises during development and declines with age-related attrition \citep[see e.g.][]{vaci2019large}, motivating a concave mean model.  Enforcing concavity also serves as a necessary regularizer against a selection artifact present in the raw data: cross-sectional averages at late ages (35+) are computed only over players still active in the league, who form a positively selected elite subset.  Figure~\ref{fig:empirical_production} (Appendix) illustrates this directly: several metrics display an apparent uptick at older ages that violates the expected decline, an artifact of only high-performing players surviving to those ages.  A flexible unconstrained model would spuriously interpret this upward bias as a genuine late-career improvement.  The concave prior prevents the model from fitting this artifact as a real trajectory feature, playing a complementary role to the survival model: the Weibull component accounts for which players we observe at all, while the concave prior constrains the shape of what we fit to those we do observe.  In Section~\ref{sec:concave_prior} we introduce the construction, then apply it jointly across all $K$ metrics.

\subsection{A Non-Parametric Prior for Concave Functions}
\label{sec:concave_prior}

In this Section, we first describe the construction of the nonparametric prior over concave functions. Our approach mimics the strategy used by \cite{convexityconstraints}, who propose a non-parametric prior over the space of increasing functions.
The prior is placed on the latent mean $\mu_{ptk}$ in the linear predictor space.  Because all link functions used in this paper (exponential for Poisson and Negative-Binomial metrics; logistic for Binomial and Beta-Binomial) are strictly monotone increasing, the location of the maximum is preserved under the inverse link: $\arg\max_t \, g^{-1}(\mu_{ptk}) = \arg\max_t \, \mu_{ptk}$.  Consequently, the peak age $t^*_k$ and the rank ordering of peak values are exactly preserved in the observable space, and the model's ability to place a structured prior on peak age and peak value is maintained regardless of observation family.


\begin{proposition}[Concave Gaussian process prior]
Define the random function $h(t)$ as 
\begin{align}
    h(t) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 (t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s [g(z)]^2 \, dz \, ds,
    \label{eqn:concave_process_uni}
\end{align}
with arbitrary constants $\beta_0, \beta_1 \in \mathbb{R}$, where $g(z)$ is a Gaussian process.
Then $h(t)$ is almost surely concave, and the push-forward measure
\begin{align*}
\nu(A) &= \mu(T^{-1}(A)) \quad \text{for measurable } A, \\
T(g) &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 (t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s g(z)^2\, dz\, ds,
\end{align*}
puts positive mass on every uniform neighborhood of any continuous concave function.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
 
Let $\mu$ denote the law of $g(t)$ as a Gaussian measure on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$.  
The map
\[
T: g \mapsto \beta_0 + \beta_1(t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s g(z)^2 dz ds
\]
is continuous from $L^2([a,b])$ to $C([a,b])$ for any compact interval $[a,b]$, because:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Squaring is continuous from $L^2 \to L^1$
    \item Double integration is bounded from $L^1 \to C([a,b])$
\end{itemize}

By standard Gaussian measure theory, $\mu$ has full support in $L^2([a,b])$ under mild conditions on the covariance function.  
Therefore, for any $h_0$ smooth concave function on $[a,b]$, if we define
\[
g_0(t) = \sqrt{-h_0''(t)} \in L^2([a,b]),
\]
then for any $\epsilon>0$,
\[
\mu(\|g-g_0\|_{L^2}<\epsilon) > 0.
\]
By continuity of $T$, this implies
\[
\nu(\|h - h_0\|_\infty < \delta) > 0
\]
for some $\delta>0$ depending on $\epsilon$.  
Since smooth concave functions are dense in the set of continuous concave functions on $[a,b]$, the pushforward measure $\nu$ assigns positive mass to every uniform neighborhood of any continuous concave function.
The argument extends locally to all compact intervals, giving the result on $\mathbb{R}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Concave Process Prior] 
A function h(t) has a \textbf{Concave Process (CP)} prior iff it has the form \ref{eqn:concave_process_uni}. We denote it as 
\begin{align}
    h(t) \sim \mathcal{CP}(\Theta)
\end{align}
where $\Theta$ indicate the parameters for $g(z)$, the underlying Gaussian Process. 

\end{definition}

We call this prior the ``concave process prior''. This process is the result of a composition of continuous push-forward maps applied to a Gaussian Process with a stationary kernel. Because this underlying process has been shown to be a universal function approximator, these continuous transformations preserve this property in the transformed space. Thus, this nonparametric construction assigns positive mass to every uniform neighborhood of any continuous concave function on a compact interval --- the precise sense in which it is a universal prior over concave functions.


In practice, the double integral in Equation \ref{eqn:concave_process_uni} cannot be evaluated analytically, so we make a linear approximation to the Gaussian process \citet{convexityconstraints}.  Specifically, we use the Hilbert Space GP approximation (HSGP) \cite{Solin_2019}. The resulting approximation represents the function $f(t)\approx \sum_{l=1}^J S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l \psi_l(t)$ as a finite, weighted, linear combination of $J$ eigenvectors, $\psi_l(t)$, of the Laplacian on the domain of $t$, with the weights $\alpha_l \sim N(0,S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}))$, arising as a function of the spectral density $S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})$ evaluated at the associated eigenvalue $\sqrt{\lambda_l}$ of the stationary kernel $\mathcal{K}$. If $\mathcal{K}$ is the standard squared exponential kernel, $S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}) = s^2\sqrt{2\pi}\omega \text{exp}(\frac{-\omega^2\lambda_l}{2})$ is the spectral density of the squared exponential with length-scale parameter $\omega$ and variance parameter $s$. This approach is useful for one-dimensional cases, where the domain $\Omega$ is bounded on  $[-L, L]$. 
In the one dimensional Dirichlet boundary condition case, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are $\lambda_j = (\frac{j \pi}{2L})^2$ and $\psi_l(t) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{L}}sin(\sqrt{\lambda_l}(L + t))$ respectively. 

This approximation allows us derive an analytic solution to the double integral and express $h(t)$ in a more tractable form, since the resulting integral involves the sinusoidal eigenfunctions. 

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:concave_uni_gp}

Assume \eqref{eqn:concave_process_uni} holds and replace $g(t) \approx \sum_{l=1}^J S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l \psi_l(t)$, where $\psi_l(t)$ are the $J$ HSGP basis functions for a stationary kernel on $t$. Then, 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:final_uni_gp}
    f^{(k)}(t | \Theta) = a + b(t+L) - \mathbf{\alpha}^T \Psi(t) \mathbf{\alpha}
\end{align} 
%
where 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:phi}
\Psi_{lz}(t) = 
\begin{cases} \frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})^2} +
\frac{(t+L)^2}{4L}  & \text{if } l = z \\
\frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})^2} - \frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{-}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{-})^2} & \text{if } l \neq z
\end{cases}
\end{align}
Here, $\mathbf{\alpha}$ is a $J$-vector with entries given by $\alpha_l \cdot S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2}$, and $\epsilon_{lz}^{+} = \sqrt{\lambda_l} + \sqrt{\lambda_z}$, $\epsilon_{lz}^{-} = \sqrt{\lambda_l} - \sqrt{\lambda_z}$, respectively. $\Theta$ indicates parameters relevant for the HSGP approximation, namely the lengthscale and variance parameters, $\omega$ and $s$. 

\noindent Proof: see Appendix \ref{appendix:univariate_concavity}.
\end{proposition}


\begin{proposition}[Convergence of the HSGP approximation]
Let $k$ be a stationary covariance function on $[0,T]$ with spectral
density $S$, and let $\{\phi_i,\lambda_i\}_{i=1}^\infty$ be the Laplacian
eigenpairs on $[0,T]$.
Define $\omega_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$.
Let
\[
g(t)
=
\sum_{i=1}^\infty
\sqrt{S(\omega_i)}\,\alpha_i \psi_i(t),
\qquad
\alpha_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1),
\]
and its truncation
\[
g_K(t)
=
\sum_{i=1}^K
\sqrt{S(\omega_i)}\,\alpha_i \psi_i(t).
\]
Define
\[
f(t)
=
\int_0^t \int_0^s g(z)^2\,dz\,ds,
\qquad
f_K(t)
=
\int_0^t \int_0^s g_K(z)^2\,dz\,ds.
\]
Then
\[
f_K \to f
\quad \text{uniformly on } [0,T] \text{ almost surely}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the HSGP construction (\cite{Solin_2019}), the covariance
operator admits the spectral approximation
\[
k(t,t')
\approx
\sum_{i=1}^K S(\omega_i)\psi_i(t)\psi_i(t').
\]
Under $\sum_i S(\omega_i)<\infty$, the Gaussian series defining $g$
converges almost surely in $L^2([0,T])$, and
\[
\mathbb{E}\|g-g_K\|_{L^2}^2
=
\sum_{i=K+1}^\infty S(\omega_i)
\to 0.
\]
Hence $g_K \to g$ almost surely in $L^2$.
Using
\[
\|g_K^2 - g^2\|_{L^1}
\le
\|g_K-g\|_{L^2}\|g_K+g\|_{L^2},
\]
we obtain $g_K^2 \to g^2$ in $L^1$ almost surely.
The double-integration operator
\[
I(h)(t)=\int_0^t\int_0^s h(z)\,dz\,ds
\]
is bounded from $L^1([0,T])$ to $C([0,T])$.
Therefore $f_K = I(g_K^2) \to I(g^2)=f$ uniformly almost surely.
\end{proof}



% \subsubsection{Reparameterizing the Concave Process}
The concave process, Equation \eqref{eq:final_uni_gp} can be reparameterized into an interpretable form that is more amenable to prior elicitation.  Specifically, note that $a$ and $b$ are variationally independent parameters that control the shape of the concave function. For modeling NBA production, we are most interesting in reasoning about both the value and age of peak performance. As such, define $f^*$ and $t^*$ the max and argmax of $f(t)$ respectively, that is

\begin{align}
f^* = \underset{t}{\text{max }} f(t) = f(t^*) 
\end{align}
%
In the following Theorem, we state the parameterization of the concave process in terms of $f^*$ and $t^*$.
%
\begin{theorem}\label{theorem:concave_max_approx}
Let $f(t | \Theta)$
%\approx D(x) + C(x)(t+L) -  \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T\mathbf{A}^T \Psi(t) \mathbf{\tilde{A}} \mathbf{\phi}(x)$ 
be defined as in Equation \eqref{eq:final_uni_gp}. Then, if $f(t | \Theta)$ takes on the maximum value of $f^*$ at time $t^*$,  

\begin{align}
\label{eq:max_constraint_gp}
    f^{(k)}(t | \Theta) = f^* + \mathbf{A}^T [ \Psi(t^*) - \Psi(t) + \frac{d\Psi(t^*)}{dt}(t - t^*) ] \mathbf{A}  
\end{align}

where $\frac{d\Psi(t^*)}{dt}$, the time derivative of \eqref{eq:phi}, can be computed analytically.
% \begin{align}
% \label{eq:phi_prime}
% \Psi^{'}_{lz}(t) = 
% \begin{cases} \frac{sin(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L))}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})} +
% \frac{(t+L)}{2L}  & \text{if } l = z \\
% \frac{sin(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L))}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})} - \frac{sin(\epsilon_{lz}^{-}(t+L))}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{-})} & \text{if } l \neq z
% \end{cases}
% \end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
See Appendix \ref{appendix:peak_value}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Prior moment properties.}
The reparameterization in Theorem~\ref{theorem:concave_max_approx} has an important consequence for the prior's variance structure.  Writing the process explicitly as
\begin{equation}
  f(t) = f^* + (t - t^*)\!\int_{t_0}^{t^*} g(z)^2\,dz
         - \int_{t}^{t^*}\!\int_{t_0}^{s} g(z)^2\,dz\,ds,
  \label{eq:f_peak_rep}
\end{equation}
shows that at $t = t^*$ both integral terms vanish identically, so the GP component contributes zero variance at the peak; all uncertainty in $f(t^*)$ comes from the prior on $f^*$ alone.  Away from the peak the dominant contribution to $\operatorname{Var}(f(t) \mid t^*, f^*)$ is the squared double integral.  For a zero-mean GP $g$ with marginal variance $\sigma^2$, the Isserlis (Wick) theorem gives $E[g(z)^2 g(z')^2] = \sigma^4 + 2\mathcal{K}(z,z')^2$.  Integrating this over the triangular domain in \eqref{eq:f_peak_rep} yields
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(f(t) \mid t^*, f^*) \;\approx\; \tfrac{3}{4}\,\sigma^4\,(t - t^*)^4,
  \label{eq:var_quartic}
\end{equation}
so variance grows quartically with distance from the peak, scaling as $\sigma^4$ rather than $\sigma^2$ as in a standard GP.  This reflects the structural difficulty of the underlying inference problem: locating the peak of a function from $n$ temporally ordered observations is inherently harder than estimating its mean.  Mode estimation from $n$ observations converges at rate $n^{-2/5}$ under standard regularity conditions, compared with $n^{-1/2}$ for the mean; for NBA careers where $n$ is typically 5--12 seasons, this gap is substantial.  The $\sigma^4$ variance growth encodes this difficulty directly, placing greatest prior precision at the peak and allowing growing uncertainty with distance from it, consistent with the intuition that career trajectories are better identified near their apex than at the sparsely-observed early or late extremes.  Practically, this also means posterior uncertainty in $t^*$ propagates outward: a player whose peak age is uncertain will have wider credible bands across the full trajectory, not just at the peak.

\subsubsection{Modeling Dependence Across Concave Processes}

In this Section, we extend the univariate concave process to a process over multiple concave functions.  Here, we assume each process is associated with auxiliary time-invariant covariates, $x$.  We let $h(x,t)$  denote a multivariate concave process associated with covariate $x$ which is concave in $t$ and model the dependence between $h(x,t)$ and $h(x',t)$ for $x \neq x'$. We extend the concave process to a multivariate processes by using a multi-output Gaussian process. 


%
% We construct this prior by noting that the following function $f(x,t)$ is concave in $t$ if and only if $\frac{\partial ^2 f(x,z)}{\partial z^2} \geq 0$.

% \begin{align}
% \label{eq:concave_multi_rep}
%     f(x,t) = a(x) + b(x)(t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s \frac{[g(x,z)]^2}dzds 
% \end{align}
%
Specifically, we can construct concave functions, $f(x,t)$, by extending \eqref{eq:final_uni_gp} by integrating the square of a multi-input Gaussian Process, $g(x,t)$ and allowing the integration constants $a$ and $b$ to also depend on $x$. Specifically, $g(x,t) \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, K_x \otimes K_t)$, where $K_x$ and $K_t$ are stationary covariance kernels acting on the $x$ space, and time $t$, independently. 
We wish to find all set of $f(x,t)$ such that $[g(x,t)] ^2 = \frac{\partial ^2 f(x,t)}{\partial t^2} \geq 0$. As such, our desired function has the form
\begin{align}
\label{eq:concave_multi_rep}
    h(x,t) = a(x) + b(x)(t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s [g(x,z)]^2dzds 
\end{align}
where $a(x), b(x)$ are some constants of integration with respect to $t$.

\begin{definition}[Multivariate Concave Process Prior] 
A function h(x, t) has a \textbf{Multivariate Concave Process (MCP)} prior iff it has the form \ref{eq:concave_multi_rep}. We denote it as 
\begin{align}
    h(x, t) \sim \mathcal{MCP}(\Theta; X)
\end{align}
where $\Theta$ denotes parameters for the underlying Gaussian process $g(x,z)$, and $X$ indicates the additional input dimensions.
\end{definition}

\begingroup\sloppy
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:concave_multi_gp}
Assume \eqref{eq:concave_multi_rep} holds and let $g(x,t) = \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x) \bigl( \sum_{l=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t)\bigr)$, where $\phi_i(x)$ is a projection of $x$ and $\psi_l(t)$ are HSGP basis functions for $t$. Then,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:final_gp}
    f^{(k,m)}(x,t| \Theta) &= f^{*}(x) \nonumber\\
    &+  \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T\mathbf{A}^T \!\left[ \Psi(t^{*}(x)) - \Psi(t) + \tfrac{d\Psi(t^*(x))}{dt} (t - t^{*}(x)) \right] \mathbf{A}\, \mathbf{\phi}(x)
\end{align}
%
where $\Psi_{lz}(t)$ is defined as in Equation \ref{eq:phi}, and $\mathbf{A}$ is a $M$ by $K$ matrix where $\mathbf{A}_{il} = S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l^i$. Our formulation  of $f(x, t | \Theta) \xrightarrow{} h(x,t)$ uniformly  \ref{eq:concave_multi_rep}
as $K, M \xrightarrow{} \infty$.

\noindent Proof: see Appendix \ref{appendix:multivariate_concavity}.
\end{proposition}
\endgroup

Much like the univariate case, $\Theta$ consists of the relevant HSGP parameters, as well as additionally, the parameters controlling the projection of $X$.


Much like the univariate case \ref{theorem:concave_max_approx},  parameters $f^{*}(x)$ and $t^{*}(x)$ can be specified as variationally independent processes, with the additional benefit that these parameters can now pool information across additional dimensions. As a result, we have now a process which can produce concavity across functions, while ensuring that these functions will behave similarly (peak value, peak location, etc.)  with respect to other input dimensions.

be defined as in Equation \eqref{eq:concave_multi_rep}. Then,

\end{align}
 where $Z(x,x^{'}, t, t^{'})$ is a non-negative function, and $\mathcal{K}(x,x^{'})$ is the covariance kernel between two inputs in the latent space. 
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Modeling Player Similarity}
\label{sec:player_similarity}

% Our second priority, now that we have formulated an approach which models concave functions over $t$ while incorporating extra information, $X$, is to share information across the player space, and allow similarities in player space to encourage learning similar aging patterns.

We now demonstrate how we apply the MCP model NBA production across players, metrics and time.  While we could use player-specific covariates to inform similarity between individuals (e.g. anthromporphic measurements, college performance, etc) we instead choose to model similarity between players in a data-informed way by treated player-specific covariates as latent variables. Models which treat the inputs to  Gaussian Processes are  referred to as a Gaussian Process Latent Variable Model (GPLVM) \cite{JMLR:v6:lawrence05a}. Traditionally, Gaussian Process regression is focused on predicting a response $y$ from a set of query points $X^{*}$, given a set of fixed input points $X$. Latent Variable GPs, however, assume that the input points $X$ are not observed, and are rather a latent variable that is to be learned. With a linear kernel, this approach resembles that of probabilistic PCA \cite{JMLR:v6:lawrence05a}. However, alternative kernel formulations offer a more flexible way to model non-linear functions, as the optimization procedure searches over the space of possible latent representations in order to find ones which are most applicable to the learning task at hand.

Rather than placing an uninformative isotropic prior on $X_p$, we encode prior domain knowledge through a \emph{structured} prior mean.  The continuous covariates $-\log d_p$ and $h_p$ are standardized to zero mean and unit variance across the roster before model fitting.  Let
\[
  Z_p = \bigl[-\log d_p,\; h_p,\;\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{pos}_p = F],\;\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{pos}_p = G]\bigr]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^4
\]
collect four player-level covariates: negative log draft position (a proxy for scouting consensus), height, and two indicator variables for positional group (Forward and Guard, with Center as the baseline category).  Players without a recorded draft position (undrafted free agents) are assigned a sentinel rank of $P{+}1$, where $P$ is the total number of players in the cohort, so that $-\log d_p$ defaults to the minimum value after standardization; survival model entry times for such players are set to their first observed NBA season.  We introduce a learned projection $W \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times q}$ with hierarchical scale $\sigma_W$ and a residual scale $\sigma_X < 1$, and place the prior
\begin{align}
  X_p &\sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(Z_p^\top W,\; \sigma_X^2\, I_q\bigr), \quad
  W \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, \sigma_W^2\, I_{4\times q}), \nonumber\\
  \sigma_W &\sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(1), \quad
  \sigma_X \sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(0.5).
\end{align}
The prior mean $Z_p^\top W$ pulls a player's latent position toward the subspace predicted by draft standing, physical size, and positional role; the residual scale $\sigma_X$ (concentrated below 1) controls how tightly the data must pull $X_p$ away from that prior mean.  When $\sigma_W \to 0$ the prior reduces to $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_X^2 I_q)$, recovering the isotropic case.  Crucially, $W$ is shared across all players, so the projection is learned from the full roster rather than treated as fixed.  Note that each process is independent across metrics, but $X$ is shared across all metrics.

\paragraph{Identifiability.}
The latent coordinates $X_p$ are identified only up to orthogonal rotation: for any orthogonal $R \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times q}$, replacing $X_p$ with $X_p R$ and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}^k$ with $R^\top \boldsymbol{\gamma}^k$ leaves $f^k(X_p, t)$ unchanged.  The posterior therefore represents an equivalence class of rotations rather than a unique coordinate frame.  Importantly, the downstream quantities of interest --- pairwise Euclidean distances (and hence nearest-neighbor retrieval), angular relationships, and Ward-linkage hierarchical clustering --- are all invariant to orthogonal rotation, so the ambiguity does not affect the scientific conclusions.  We discuss the practical resolution of this ambiguity via Procrustes alignment in Section~\ref{sec:inference}.

For a full treatment on GPLVMs, please consult \cite{bodin2017latentgaussianprocessregression}.

\section{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

Inference for this model presents some unique challenges specific to the formulation. Bayesian factor models typically have rotational identifiability issues with either the loadings or the factors themselves, yielding a multi-modal posterior. This makes traditional Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) challenging. We therefore initialize the MCMC chains from the MAP estimate of the full model in Section~\ref{sec:proposed_model}, which concentrates the sampler near a well-identified mode before full sampling begins.

The MAP estimate is used to fix a small set of kernel hyperparameters whose posterior is well-determined by the full data: specifically, the concave GP lengthscale and amplitude ($\alpha$, $l_k$, $\sigma_t$, $\sigma_c$), the structured prior scales ($\sigma_W$, $\sigma_X$), and the Weibull scale offset parameters. All remaining parameters are sampled freely by MCMC: the latent embeddings $X_p$ for all players, the HSGP curve weights $\beta$, the per-metric peak parameters ($c_{\max}$, $t_{\max}^{\text{raw}}$), and the full AR(1) components ($\rho^k$, $\sigma^k_{\mathrm{AR}}$, $\mathbf{r}^k_p$). Fixing only hyperparameters while sampling all structural parameters follows the factor pre-training approach of \cite{mauri2025factorpretrainingbayesianmultivariate} and substantially reduces the multi-modality arising from rotational non-identifiability of the latent space. 

The MAP estimation and MCMC sampling were conducted in NumPyro with a GPU, with the MAP estimate obtained by Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) and MCMC samples acquired using No U-Turn Sampling (NUTS). Posterior samples were obtained from 4 chains, each running 2{,}000 post-warmup iterations (1{,}000 warmup) and thinned by a factor of 8, yielding 250 retained draws per chain (1{,}000 total); the injury variant uses 50 draws per chain (200 total) due to its higher computational cost. Full $\hat{R}$ diagnostics are in Appendix~\ref{sec:convergence}; for the predictive mean $\mu_{ptk}$, the fraction of (player $\times$ age) cells with $\hat{R} > 1.05$ ranges from 7.5\% (STL, USG) to 40.8\% (AST), with 16 of 17 metrics below 26\%. The elevated AST fraction reflects genuine mixing difficulty: assist production is highly player-specific and more sensitive to latent factor uncertainty than other count metrics, making between-chain agreement harder to achieve within the current chain budget. A latent dimension of $q = 10$ was used for the player embedding space, along with 20 HSGP features, to approximate the CPLVM. The choice of $q$ was informed by a holdout ablation study (Appendix~\ref{sec:ablation_q}) comparing $q \in \{5, 10, 15, 20\}$ on the hold-out last-$k$ scheme; $q = 10$ achieved the lowest mean holdout RMSE (0.924 vs.\ 0.993, 1.016, 0.968 for $q = 5, 15, 20$) and the lowest mean log-loss (3.99), winning on 11 of 17 metrics by log-loss, while $q = 15$ and $q = 20$ showed signs of over-parameterization with degraded distributional calibration.

\paragraph{Procrustes alignment for posterior analysis.}
Because the latent embedding $X_p \in \mathbb{R}^q$ is only identified up to orthogonal rotation of its learned coordinates, raw MCMC draws from different chains occupy different rotation frames and cannot be directly compared or averaged.  To enable coherent posterior summaries, we apply Procrustes alignment to each (chain, draw) pair: given the MAP estimate $\hat{X}$ as a fixed reference, we find the orthogonal matrix $R^{(s)}$ minimising $\|\hat{X} - X^{(s)} R^{(s)}\|_F$ via singular value decomposition, and replace each sampled $X^{(s)}$ with the aligned version $X^{(s)} R^{(s)}$ before any downstream computation.  All posterior quantities reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} --- nearest-neighbor tables, archetype clustering, and embedding uncertainty --- are derived from these Procrustes-aligned samples.  Convergence diagnostics for the aligned draws are shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:convergence}.

\paragraph{Posterior predictive generation.}
Many metrics are conditional on each other through the exposure structure: counting statistics depend on minutes played, which in turn depends on games played.  Posterior predictive curves are therefore generated in three stages.  \textbf{Stage 1}: games played (GP\%) is sampled from its posterior predictive distribution.  \textbf{Stage 2}: minutes per game (MPG) is sampled conditionally, with the stage-1 games draw used to form the total-minutes exposure.  \textbf{Stage 3}: all counting and rate metrics (FTA, AST, DREB, etc.) are sampled using the composite minutes from stages 1--2 as their exposure offset; shooting percentages are then derived from the sampled counts.  This cascade propagates exposure uncertainty forward: if the stage-1 draw yields zero games, all downstream metrics are zero, encoding the possibility that the player has exited the league.  The model thereby integrates production uncertainty with career-length uncertainty within a single posterior draw, which is the primary operational value for front-office forecasting.

\subsection{The Benefits of Joint Modeling}
A key advantage of the joint model is its ability to borrow information across metrics and across similar players.
Even for an established star with many seasons of data, a single-metric AR(1) model treats each metric in isolation and cannot capture the cross-metric coherence that defines a player's archetype.
The joint model pools information from all 17 metrics simultaneously: the shared latent embedding ties together rebounding, playmaking, and scoring into a single player representation, producing tighter and more coherent posteriors. By ELPPD (Section~\ref{sec:validation}), the Concave$+$AR model significantly outperforms the AR baseline on count statistics (BLK, STL, DREB, OREB; all $|\Delta|>1.96$\,SE), where the bounded career arc provides regularization unavailable to a pure AR process.

The \emph{latent-space} distance operates in the GPLVM embedding $X_p \in \mathbb{R}^q$: two players are close if their entire 17-metric production profile is globally similar after dimensionality reduction, which is why Joki\'{c}'s latent neighbors are all high-usage, multi-metric playmakers (Luka Don\v{c}i\'{c}, Arvydas and Alperen Sengun) who share his unusual combination of elite rebounding, elite playmaking, and elite scoring.

\paragraph{Predictive accuracy.}
We evaluate predictive accuracy under four complementary holdout schemes, each designed to stress-test a different aspect of the model (Table~\ref{tab:holdout_schemes}; full results in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_validation}).
All schemes randomly select 20\% of players and withhold their observations under different rules:
\emph{Hold-out Last $k$} ($k=2$; 727 obs) withholds each selected player's final 2 seasons, testing \textbf{prospective extrapolation} --- the primary front-office use case of forecasting near-future production;
\emph{Hold-out First $k$} ($k=2$; 757 obs) withholds the first 2 seasons, testing \textbf{early-career recovery} from later-career data;
\emph{Random Interior} (413 obs) withholds a random 20\% of each player's interior seasons, testing \textbf{mid-career interpolation}; and
\emph{Hold-out Peak} (450 obs) withholds the single peak-production season per metric, directly testing \textbf{peak estimation accuracy}.
We compare four models across all schemes: an AR(1)-only baseline (\emph{AR}), the Concave process without temporal dependence (\emph{Concave}), the joint Concave$+$AR model, and the full Concave$+$AR$+$Injury model.

The proposed model is not designed to uniformly dominate the AR baseline on every predictive metric; its value lies in the structural inference it enables alongside broadly comparable holdout performance.
By RMSE, the Concave prior is competitive or superior across all metrics without requiring serial dependence; adding the AR(1) component does not improve point prediction accuracy (AST: 2.03 vs.\ 1.77 for Concave alone; DREB: 1.73 vs.\ 1.64; BLK: 0.75 vs.\ 0.63).
By ELPPD (Table~\ref{tab:elppd_holdout_last_k}), the picture is asymmetric in an important way.  Where the structured models are better, they are \emph{significantly} better: the Concave$+$AR model achieves significantly higher ELPPD than the AR baseline on 11 of the 12 metrics where it is the leading model (all $|\Delta|>1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$), including all count statistics (BLK, STL, DREB, OREB, AST, TOV, FTA, FG2A, FG3A) and shooting percentages (FG2\%, FG3\%), with gains ranging from $0.12$ to $5.15$ ELPPD per observation.  Where the AR baseline leads --- GP\%, USG\%, OBPM, DBPM, and MPG --- the advantage is almost entirely \emph{not} statistically significant: OBPM and DBPM show no significant difference from any model variant, USG\% is significant only against the Injury variant, and GP\% is not significantly different from the pure Concave model.  MPG is the lone exception where the AR baseline is consistently and significantly better across all three structured variants, reflecting its persistence assumption at career endpoints where minutes allocation is driven by roster decisions rather than the aging arc the model is designed to capture.
By holdout log-loss, the AR(1) component improves calibration on volume counting statistics (AST: 10.25 vs.\ 14.47 for Concave alone; DREB: 6.84 vs.\ 8.30; TOV: 5.22 vs.\ 5.69), and the Concave$+$AR$+$Injury model achieves the best log-loss on AST (9.75) and FTA (8.58).
Critically, the AR model has no mechanism to represent the bounded, concave arc of a career: it cannot produce posterior estimates of peak age or peak value, embed players in a shared latent space for similarity retrieval, or support counterfactual inference about injury impact --- structural contributions that are orthogonal to any holdout comparison.

The AR model's competitiveness on Hold-out Last $k$ reflects a structural asymmetry in the estimation problem: predicting the next season for a player already observed through age 28 reduces to tracking a local trend, which an AR(1) handles well without ever committing to a peak location.  Locating the peak itself from sparse temporal data is the harder problem, as the $\sigma^4$ prior variance structure in Section~\ref{sec:concave_prior} makes precise.  This asymmetry is visible in the complementary holdout schemes: on Hold-out First $k$ and Hold-out Peak, where the AR model must extrapolate backward or identify the maximum without having observed it, the Concave$+$AR model achieves consistently lower MAP log-loss across metrics (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_validation}).  These schemes directly test the structural inference the concave prior is designed for, and the gap with the AR baseline is widest there.



\subsection{Uncovering Metric Relationships}

We then perform Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the resultant matrix, and plot the loadings for each of the metrics for the first two principal components. \subsection{Modality-Specific Archetype Clustering}
Each modality-specific embedding is derived from the bilinear structure of the model.  For a given modality $m$ (peak timing, peak value, or curvature basis $m$), the player-metric effect is $X_p^\top \boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k,(m)}$, where $\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k,(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^q$ is the per-metric weight vector drawn from the posterior.  We rescale each latent dimension $j$ of $X_p$ by the posterior standard deviation of $\gamma^{k,(m)}_j$ across all $K$ metrics, forming the modality-specific coordinate
\[
  \tilde{X}_{pj}^{(m)} \;=\; X_{pj} \cdot \mathrm{std}_{k}\!\bigl(\gamma^{k,(m)}_j\bigr).
\]
This operation siphons the learned feature importance from the weight matrix into the latent coordinates: a dimension $j$ whose weight varies substantially across metrics is strongly modality-discriminating and is amplified; a dimension whose weight is nearly constant across metrics contributes uniformly to all metrics and is suppressed.  The raw archetype clustering of Section~\ref{sec:latent_space} uses $X_p$ directly, treating all latent dimensions symmetrically and remaining agnostic to any particular facet of the career arc.

The rescaled coordinates $\tilde{X}^{(m)}$ are Procrustes-aligned across posterior draws and subjected to the same Ward hierarchical clustering. \subsection{Early Identification of Peak Performers and Draft Evaluation}
A central question for front offices is how quickly the model can resolve a player's peak age and peak value from limited early-career data.  Because $X_p$ is inferred from the full longitudinal metric profile and peak timing is encoded directly in the concave trajectory $f^k(X_p, t)$, the posterior over both peak age $t^*_{pk}$ and peak value $f^*_{pk}$ contracts as more seasons are observed.  For a rookie with one or two seasons of data, the posteriors are wide: the model registers genuine uncertainty about where the career arc will crest.  By the third or fourth season --- once above-replacement minutes accumulate and the trajectory's curvature becomes discernible --- the posteriors concentrate substantially, and archetype assignment stabilises.

The structured prior on $X_p$ (Equation~\ref{eq:structured_prior}) anchors this process: draft standing, height, and positional role provide an informative starting point, so that the model does not require five seasons of evidence before forming a view.  Posterior uncertainty has a direct front-office interpretation --- a wide credible region for $f^*_{pk}$ indicates that a player's ceiling is not yet determined by the data, while a narrow region indicates confident placement.  A breakout season shifts the posterior mean of $X_p$ substantially toward the elite archetype, whereas consistent-but-unremarkable production converges slowly.  Convergence to a stable archetype assignment typically requires 2--3 seasons of above-replacement minutes, consistent with conventional scouting timelines for evaluating prospect development.

We operationalise this as a draft-cohort evaluation tool using four posterior summaries per player: posterior mean peak OBPM; $P(\text{OBPM}{>}2)$, $P(\text{OBPM}{>}3)$, and $P(\text{OBPM}{>}4)$ — the fraction of posterior peak-value draws exceeding each threshold; and expected years to peak, the posterior mean OBPM peak age minus the player's last observed age.  Empirically, a career-peak OBPM of 2 corresponds to roughly the 81st percentile of NBA players, 3 to the 88th, and 4 to the 93rd, so these thresholds span the range from reliable above-average starter to All-Star calibre production.  Table~\ref{tab:breakout_cohort} reports the top five players per entry cohort ranked by posterior mean peak OBPM.  Posterior confidence grows sharply with seasons observed: the 2021--2022 cohorts show high mean peak OBPM for their top players (Zion Williamson, Tyrese Haliburton, LaMelo Ball) with near-certain $P(\text{OBPM}{>}2)$, while the 2026 cohort, with only one season of data, yields more diffuse probabilities.  Victor Wembanyama appears prominently in the 2025 cohort with a mean peak OBPM of 3.8 and $P(\text{OBPM}{>}2) = 99\%$, reflecting the model's rapid posterior contraction after an exceptional rookie season; his years-to-peak estimate is near zero, consistent with the model identifying his first season as already close to his projected prime.  Figure~\ref{fig:breakout_cohort_dist} shows the full within-cohort distributions; the narrowing spread from 2021 to 2026 directly reflects posterior contraction as career data accumulates.

\subsection{Injury Effect Estimation}

To move beyond individual case studies and quantify injury impact at the population level, we augment the concave mean model with injury-specific offsets.  For player $p$ with injury subtype $m$ onset at season $t^{*}_{pm}$, the mean model becomes:
\begin{align}
    \tau^{*}_k &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d), \quad w_m \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d) \\
    \tau^k_{ptm} &\sim \mathcal{N}(w_m^\top\tau^{*}_k,\; \sigma^2_{mk})  \\
    \mu^k_{ptm} &= f^k(X_p,t) + \mathbb{I}\{ t > t^{*}_{pm} \}\,\tau^k_{ptm},
\end{align}
where $\tau^{*}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are metric-level latent injury factors, $w_m \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are injury-subtype loadings, $\sigma^2_{mk}$ is a metric- and subtype-specific variance, and $d = 5$ is the injury factor dimension.  Players without the relevant injury subtype have $t^{*}_{pm} = \infty$ and contribute to the baseline.

Figure~\ref{fig:injury_global} shows the posterior distribution of the global injury offset averaged across metrics and subtypes.  The offset is consistently negative, confirming that the model identifies a systematic post-injury production decline beyond what is explained by the aging curve alone.  Figure~\ref{fig:injury_type} breaks this down by injury subtype and metric, while Figure~\ref{fig:injury_survival} shows how the survival model translates injury type into differences in career length.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced a joint Bayesian model for multi-metric NBA career trajectories that combines three novel components: a Concave Process (CP) prior over production curves that encodes the biological constraint of concave aging, a Gaussian process latent variable model (GPLVM) that embeds all 17 metrics into a shared low-dimensional player representation, and a survival-adjusted injury submodel that enables counterfactual inference about career arcs absent specific injury events.

\paragraph{Key quantitative findings.}
Under the holdout-last-$k$ evaluation scheme (727 player-seasons, 2022--2025), the Concave model achieves broadly comparable predictive performance to the AR baseline: by RMSE, the Concave prior is competitive or superior across all metrics; by ELPPD, the AR baseline retains an advantage on persistent rate statistics (USG\%, MPG, OBPM, DBPM) where its persistence assumption is naturally well-suited, while the Concave$+$AR model significantly outperforms on count statistics (BLK, STL, DREB, OREB; $|\Delta|>1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$).
The Concave$+$AR$+$Injury model achieves the best log-loss on AST (9.75 vs.\ 10.25 for Concave$+$AR) and FTA (8.58), confirming that injury structure primarily affects high-volume counting statistics.
The primary contribution beyond predictive parity is structural: the AR model cannot produce posterior estimates of peak age or peak value, embed players in a shared latent space, or support counterfactual injury inference --- capabilities that are orthogonal to any holdout comparison and constitute the core scientific offering of the paper.
The shared latent embedding supports principled player similarity retrieval: for Nikola Joki\'{c}, the GPLVM latent space recovers high-usage pass-first bigs (Arvydas and Domantas Sabonis, Giannis Antetokounmpo), while the Fr\'{e}chet curve distance recovers players whose aggregate trajectory shape matches Joki\'{c}'s regardless of position.
Peak-age PCA reveals interpretable metric groupings --- athleticism-driven metrics (OREB, BLK) peak before age 25, skill-based metrics (shooting efficiency, playmaking) peak around 26--28 --- providing actionable guidance for player development timelines.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Several limitations should be noted.
First, the model is estimated on players who appeared in at least one NBA game, introducing survivorship bias: players who failed to reach or sustain NBA careers are unobserved, so the posteriors describe the distribution over NBA-caliber trajectories rather than the full talent distribution.
Second, the holdout coverage analysis reveals calibration heterogeneity across metrics (60--99\% vs.\ the nominal 95\%), with OBPM showing the largest shortfall; richer non-Gaussian likelihoods or heavier-tailed error models could improve calibration for the most volatile metrics.
Third, the injury causal estimates rest on the assumption of no unmeasured confounding conditional on latent player quality, observable covariates, and career history --- an assumption that is strong and fundamentally untestable.
Fourth, the injury counterfactual analysis conditions on the first observed major injury and selects only players who returned to play afterward; players who retired immediately after injury are excluded.  This induces immortal-time bias: the estimated effects describe the subset of players robust enough to return and may understate the total career impact of the most severe injuries.
Finally, the current GP latent variable model uses a linear kernel ($\phi(X_p) = X_p$), which may not capture nonlinear interactions between player archetypes and metric trajectories.

\paragraph{Future directions.}
Several extensions are natural.
The most immediate is to apply the framework to other professional sports leagues where longitudinal performance data are available (e.g., soccer, hockey, baseball), testing whether the concave aging prior and GPLVM structure generalize beyond basketball.
A richer injury submodel --- for example, one that explicitly models injury severity, recovery time, and metric-specific damage profiles --- could improve counterfactual precision and support more granular roster management decisions.
On the inference side, the current MAP-initialized MCMC approach is computationally intensive; variational approximations or normalizing-flow posteriors for the latent space could enable real-time player evaluations as new season data arrive.
Finally, integrating the trajectory model with contract-length and salary-cap data would allow front offices to assess not just athletic value but economic value over the duration of a proposed contract.

\subsection{Model Validation}
\label{sec:appendix_validation}

The four holdout schemes are implemented by selecting a random 20\% subset of players and masking their observations as follows.

\begin{description}
  \item[Hold-out Last $k$ ($k{=}2$, 727 obs).] The final 2 observed seasons of each selected player are excluded from the training likelihood. The model therefore sees the player's career up to but not including their most recent 2 seasons, then is scored on those withheld seasons. This is the most operationally relevant scheme: it mimics the real task of forecasting a player's next 1--2 seasons from their career to date.

  \item[Hold-out First $k$ ($k{=}2$, 757 obs).] The first 2 observed seasons of each selected player are withheld. The model sees seasons 3 onward and must recover the player's rookie and sophomore output. This tests whether the concave prior and latent embedding generalise backward in time and whether the model is well-calibrated for players with sparse early-career histories.

  \item[Random Interior (413 obs).] For each selected player, a random 20\% of their interior seasons (all but the first and last) are withheld. Unlike the last-$k$ and first-$k$ schemes, the model has observations on both sides of every gap, so this tests \emph{interpolation} rather than extrapolation. It simulates partial seasons due to injury or data censoring.

  \item[Hold-out Peak (450 obs).] The single season of peak observed production for each selected player is withheld. This directly evaluates whether the model's explicit peak parameterisation ($t^*$, $f^*$) allows it to recover the peak even when it is unobserved, which is the hardest interpolation task and the one most sensitive to misspecification of the concave prior.
\end{description}

\subsection{Hilbert Space Gaussian Processes}
\label{appendix:HSGPs}
Traditionally, sampling from a full Gaussian Process is a computationally expensive procedure, involving inverting a typically large $N \times N$ covariance matrix $K$. \cite{Solin_2019} showed, however, that a reduced rank approximation of traditional stationary covariance matrices can be utilized instead, reducing computational burden. The resulting approximation represents the function $f(x)\approx \sum_{l=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l \psi_l(x)$ as a finite, weighted, linear combination of eigenvectors, $\psi_l(x)$, of the Laplacian on the domain of $x$, with the weights $\alpha_l \sim N(0,S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}))$, arising as a function of the spectral density $S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})$ evaluated at the associated eigenvalue $\sqrt{\lambda_l}$ of the stationary covariance matrix $K$. If $\mathcal{K}$ is the standard squared exponential kernel, $S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}) = s^2\sqrt{2\pi}\omega \text{exp}(\frac{-\omega^2\lambda_l}{2})$ is the spectral density of the squared exponential with length-scale parameter $\omega$ and variance parameter $s$. As we increase the number of dimensions, this approximation does not scale well, as eigenvalues for each d-tuple are needed. As such, this approach is useful for one-dimensional cases, where the domain $\Omega$ is bounded on  $[-L, L]$. 
In the one dimensional Dirichlet boundary condition case, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are $\lambda_j = (\frac{j \pi}{2L})^2$ and $\psi_l(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{L}}sin(\sqrt{\lambda_l}(L + x))$ respectively. 


For a full treatment of this approach, please refer to \cite{Solin_2019}.  


\subsection{Approximating a Separable Kernel}
\label{appendix:separability_properties}

We make the claim that
$g(x,t) \approx \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x) ( \sum_{l=1}^K \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t))$ has a separable covariance structure in $x$ and $t$. That is, $Cov( g(x,t) , g(x^{'},t^{'} ))$ can be factored into a product of kernels $K(t,t^{'})K(x,x^{'})$. 

First, we assume that $\alpha_l^i \sim N(0, S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}))$. Furthermore,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_separable}
Cov(\alpha_l^i, \alpha_z^k) = 
\begin{cases}
S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})& \text{if} \quad l = z, k = i \\
0 & \text{o.w}
\end{cases}
\end{align}
These assumptions align with the formulation outlined in \cite{Solin_2019}.

First, we will evaluate $Cov(\beta_i(t), \beta_i(t^{'}))$,
where $\beta_i(t) = \sum_{l=1}^K \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t)$.

\begin{align}
    Cov(\beta_i(t), \beta_i(t^{'})) = Cov(\sum_{l=1}^K \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t), \sum_{l=1}^K \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t^{'})) 
\end{align}
Using \eqref{eq:cov_separable}, we reach that 
\begin{align}
    Cov(\beta_i(t), \beta_i(t^{'})) = \sum_{l=1}^K  \psi_l(t^{'}) \psi_l(t) S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})
\end{align}
From \cite{Solin_2019}, $\sum_{l=1}^K  \psi_l(t^{'}) \psi_l(t) S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}) = \tilde{K}(t,t^{'})$ converges to $ K(t, t^{'})$ uniformly in the univariate case. Since we only consider the time dimension, this suffices for our purposes. 

Now, we can evaluate $Cov( g(x,t) , g(x^{'},t^{'} ))$.
\begin{align}
    Cov( g(x,t) , g(x^{'},t^{'} )) = Cov(\sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x)  \beta_i(t) , \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x^{'})  \beta_i(t^{'}) )
\end{align}
Note that from \eqref{eq:cov_separable},
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_separable_2}
Cov(\beta_i(t), \beta_j(t)) = 
\begin{cases}
\sum_{l=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_l}) \psi_l(t)^2& \text{if} \quad j = i \\
0 & \text{o.w}
\end{cases}
\end{align}
Thus,
\begin{align}
    Cov\!\left( g(x,t),\, g(x^{'},t^{'}) \right)
    &= \sum_{i=1}^M \phi(x)\phi(x^{'}) Cov\!\left(\beta_i(t), \beta_i(t^{'})\right) \nonumber\\
    &= \tilde{K}(t,t') \sum_{i=1}^M \phi(x)\phi(x^{'})
\end{align}
{\sloppy In our implementation, $\phi_i(x)$ is a linear (identity) projection, so $\sum_{i=1}^M \phi(x)\phi(x^{'}) = K_{\mathrm{lin}}(x,x^{'})$ exactly. The product $\tilde{K}(t,t^{'}) K_{\mathrm{lin}}(x,x^{'})$ therefore gives an exact separable covariance structure in the $x$ dimension, while the HSGP approximation $\tilde{K}(t,t^{'})$ converges uniformly to $K(t,t^{'})$ from \cite{Solin_2019}.\par}

\subsection{Enforcing Concavity}
\label{appendix:convexity}
\subsubsection{Univariate Case}
\label{appendix:univariate_concavity}
Considering we only wish our function $f(t)$ to be concave in the $t$ argument, we wish to find all set of $f(t)$ such that $\frac{\partial ^2 f(t)}{\partial t^2} \geq 0$. As such, our desired function has the form
\begin{align}
\label{eq:concave_uni_rep}
    f(t) = a + b(t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s \frac{\partial ^2 f(z)}{\partial z^2}dzds 
\end{align}
where $a, b$ are some constants of integration with respect to $t$. We show in the subsequent section how these constants of integration can be defined to modulate the shape of $f(t)$. 


Following the line of reasoning from \cite{convexityconstraints}, we assume that $\frac{\partial ^2 f(t)}{\partial t^2}$ can be modeled by $[g(t)]^2$, where $g(t) \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, K_t)$. 
In order to enforce convexity across the $t$ dimension, we use the Hilbert Space Gaussian Process (HSGP) approximation mentioned in \cite{convexityconstraints} to approximate $g(t) \approx \sum_{l=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l \psi_l(t)$.

Consequently,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:deriv_approx_uni_gp}
g(t)^2 \approx \sum_{l=1}^K \sum_{z=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_z})^{1/2} S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l \alpha_z \psi_l(t) \psi_z(t)\nonumber \\
 \approx  \sum_{l=1}^K \sum_{z=1}^K\tilde{\alpha_l} \tilde{\alpha_z} \psi_l(t)\psi_z(t) 
\end{align}

after letting $\tilde{\alpha}_l = S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l $.
As such, substituting \eqref{eq:deriv_approx_uni_gp} into \eqref{eq:concave_uni_rep} gives
\begin{align}
    & f(t) \approx a + b(t-t_0) - \sum_{l=1}^K \sum_{z=1}^K\tilde{\alpha_l} \tilde{\alpha_z}  \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s \psi_l(w)\psi_z(w)  dwds
\end{align}
 

Rewriting the above in terms of matrix products, and setting $t_0 = -L$,  yields
\begin{align}
\label{eq:final_gp_app_uni}
    f(t) \approx a + b(t+L) -  \mathbf{\tilde{\alpha}}^T \Psi(t) \mathbf{\tilde{\alpha}} 
\end{align} 


Here, $\mathbf{\tilde{\alpha}}$ is a $K$ vector with entries given by $\tilde{\alpha_l}$. 
The entries of the $K \times K$ matrix $\Psi(t)$ are given by
\begin{align}
\label{eq:phi_app}
\Psi_{lz}(t) =
\begin{cases} \frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})^2} +
\frac{(t+L)^2}{4L}  & \text{if } l = z \\
\frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})^2} - \frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{-}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{-})^2} & \text{if } l \neq z
\end{cases}
\end{align}

Here, $\epsilon_{lz}^{+} = \sqrt{\lambda_l} + \sqrt{\lambda_z}$, and $\epsilon_{lz}^{-} = \sqrt{\lambda_l} - \sqrt{\lambda_z}$. Note that since the eigen-functions $\psi_l(t)$ are sinusoidal, the integral in \eqref{eq:concave_uni_rep} can be computed in closed form. 


The concavity prior is placed on the latent mean $\mu_{ptk}$ in the linear predictor space; it does not, in general, guarantee concavity of the observable response after applying the inverse link.  However, the scientifically relevant properties are fully preserved.  All link functions used in the model --- exponential (Poisson, Negative-Binomial), logistic (Binomial, Beta-Binomial), and identity (Gaussian) --- are strictly monotone increasing.  For any strictly monotone $g^{-1}$,
\[
  \arg\max_t \; g^{-1}(\mu_{ptk}(t)) \;=\; \arg\max_t \; \mu_{ptk}(t),
\]
so the peak age $t^*_k$ is identical in the latent and observable spaces.  The peak value in the observable space, $g^{-1}(\mu_{ptk}(t^*_k))$, is a monotone function of the latent peak, so the rank ordering of peak values across players is also preserved.  The primary inferential targets of the model --- peak age, peak value, and the unimodal shape of the career arc --- are therefore exactly preserved under the link transformation, regardless of observation family.

\subsubsection{Multivariate Case}
\label{appendix:multivariate_concavity}
Considering we only wish our function $f(x,t)$ to be concave in the $t$ argument, but still incorporate information from another set of dimensions encoded by $x$, we wish to find all set of $f(x,t)$ such that $\frac{\partial ^2 f(x,t)}{\partial t^2} \geq 0$. As such, our desired function has the form
\begin{align}
\label{eq:concave_multi_rep_app}
    f(x,t) = a(x) + b(x)(t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s \frac{\partial ^2 f(x,z)}{\partial z^2}dzds
\end{align}
where $a(x), b(x)$ are some constants of integration with respect to $t$. We show in the subsequent section how these constants of integration can be defined to modulate the shape of $f(x,t)$.


Following the line of reasoning from \cite{convexityconstraints}, we assume that $\frac{\partial ^2 f(x,t)}{\partial t^2}$ can be modeled by $[g(x,t)]^2$, where $g(x,t) \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, K_x \otimes K_t)$. Note that this assumes that the time variable $t$ is separate from our $x$ variable . 

Using a linear projection $\phi_i(x)$ for the $x$ dimension (the identity map in our implementation), we can write $g(x,t) \approx \sum_{i=1}^M \beta_i(t) \phi_i(x)$. Here, $\beta_i(t)$ are $t$-dependent coefficients of the basis expansion. In order to enforce convexity across the $t$ dimension, we use the Hilbert Space Gaussian Process (HSGP) approximation mentioned in \cite{convexityconstraints} to approximate $\beta_i(t) \approx \sum_{l=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t)$. Note that this approximate representation preserves the separability of the covariance structure of our desired $g(x,t)$
\ref{appendix:separability_properties}.

If we let $\tilde{\alpha_l^i} = \alpha_l^i S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2}$  , then $g(x,t) \approx \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x) ( \sum_{l=1}^K \tilde{\alpha_l^i} \psi_l(t))$. 

Consequently,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:deriv_approx_gp}
g(x,t)^2 \approx \sum_{i=1}^M \sum_{j=1}^M \phi_j(x)\phi_i(x) \beta_j(t) \beta_i(t) \nonumber \\
\approx \sum_{i=1}^M \sum_{j=1}^M \phi_j(x)\phi_i(x) (\sum_{l=1}^K \tilde{\alpha_l^i} \psi_l(t)) (\sum_{l=1}^K \tilde{\alpha_l^j} \psi_l(t)) \nonumber \\  
 \approx \sum_{i=1}^M \sum_{j=1}^M \phi_j(x)\phi_i(x) (\sum_{l=1}^K \sum_{z=1}^K\tilde{\alpha_l^j} \tilde{\alpha_z^i} \psi_l(t)\psi_z(t) ) 
\end{align}

As such, substituting \eqref{eq:deriv_approx_gp} into \eqref{eq:concave_multi_rep} gives
\begin{align}
    & f(x,t) \approx a(x) + b(x)(t-t_0) -  \nonumber \\
    & \sum_{i=1}^M \sum_{j=1}^M \phi_j(x)\phi_i(x) (\sum_{l=1}^K \sum_{z=1}^K\tilde{\alpha_l^j} \tilde{\alpha_z^i}  \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s \psi_l(w)\psi_z(w)  dwds)
\end{align}
 

Rewriting the above in terms of matrix products, and setting $t_0 = -L$,  yields
\begin{align}
\label{eq:final_gp_app}
    f(x,t) \approx a(x) + b(x)(t+L) -  \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T\mathbf{\tilde{A}}^T \Psi(t) \mathbf{\tilde{A}} \mathbf{\phi}(x)
\end{align} 


Here, $\mathbf{\tilde{A}}$ is a $M \times K$ matrix with entries given by $\tilde{\alpha_l^j}$. 
The entries of the $K \times K$ matrix $\Psi(t)$ are given by \ref{eq:phi} from the univariate case. 




\subsubsection{Controlling the Peak Behavior}
\label{appendix:peak_value}
We may wish to place a prior on the location of the peak of our curve, as well as the value that this peak takes. Thus, our formulation begins with the following constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $f(x,t_{max}) = f_{max}$
    \item $\frac{d}{dt} f(x,t) |_{t = t_{max}} = 0$
\end{enumerate}

The first expectation illustrates that at our peak $t_{max}$, our function takes on the value $c$. The second expectation requires that our first derivative with respect to $t$ is 0 at $t_{max}$. Since our function is concave by construction, we do not need to constrain the behavior of the second derivative. Note here that $t_{max}$ and $f_{max}$ are specified apriori.


Taking the partial derivative of \eqref{eq:final_gp} with respect to $t$, and setting it equal to 0 yields the following requirement
\begin{align}
\label{eq:first_deriv_constraint}
    b(x) = \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T\mathbf{\tilde{A}}^T \Psi^{'}(t_{max}) \mathbf{\tilde{A}} \mathbf{\phi}(x)
\end{align}
Here, $\Psi^{'}$ is the first derivative of the entries of \eqref{eq:phi} with respect to $t$. 
Now, it remains to satisfy the peak value constraint. Formally,

\begin{align}
\label{eq:peak_value_constraint}
    a(x) = f_{max} - b(x)(t_{max} - t_0) + \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T \mathbf{\tilde{A}}^T \Psi(t_{max}) \mathbf{\tilde{A}} \mathbf{\phi}(x)
\end{align}
If we combine \eqref{eq:first_deriv_constraint} and \eqref{eq:peak_value_constraint}, and plug the resulting values for $D(x)$ and $C(x)$ into \eqref{eq:final_gp}, we are met with the following theorem.

\begin{align}
\label{eq:max_constraint_gp_app}
    f(x,t) \approx f_{max}  +  \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T\mathbf{\tilde{A}}^T [ \Psi(t_{max}) - \Psi(t) + \Psi^{'}(t_{max})(t - t_{max}) ] \mathbf{\tilde{A}} \mathbf{\phi}(x)
\end{align}
Note that when $t = t_{max}$, we recover $f_{max}$ as desired.

Subsequently, letting $f_{max}(x) , t_{max}(x) \sim GP(0, K_x)$ allows us to pool information across the $x$ dimension, allowing similar players to have similar peaks / peak-values.


\subsection{Convexity Formulation Properties}
\label{appendix:convexity_properties}
\subsubsection{Separability}
We wish to understand how the convexity constraints affect the overall separability of our process. We will assume the formulation presented in \ref{sec:proposed_model}, where the maximum location $t_{max}$ and maximum value $f_{max}$ are specified. 
{\sloppy We also assume that $g(x,t) \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, K_x \otimes K_t)$, so that $Cov(g(x,t), g(x^{'},t^{'})) = K(x,x^{'})K(t,t^{'})$, and that $g(x,t)$ has marginal variance $\sigma^2$.\par}

Initially, we first aim to ascertain $Cov(g(x,t)^2,g(x^{'},t^{'})^2)$.
We can rewrite the covariance as the following through the definition of covariance:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:cov_1}
    &Cov\!\left(g(x,t)^2,\,g(x^{'},t^{'})^2\right) \nonumber\\
    &\quad = E[g(x,t)g(x,t)g(x^{'},t^{'})g(x^{'},t^{'})] - E[g(x^{'},t^{'})^2]\,E[g(x,t)^2]
\end{align}


We can rewrite the first expectation using a special case of \cite{isserlis} Theorem as the following:

\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_2}
E[g(x,t)g(x,t)g(x^{'},t^{'})g(x^{'},t^{'})] = \nonumber \\ E[g(x^{'},t^{'})^2]E[g(x,t)^2] + 2E[g(x,t)g(x^{'},t^{'})]^2 
\end{align}

Inserting \eqref{eq:cov_2} into \eqref{eq:cov_1}, we see that 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_3}
    Cov(g(x,t)^2,g(x^{'},t^{'})^2) = \nonumber \\ 2E[g(x,t)g(x^{'},t^{'})]^2 = \nonumber\\ 2Cov(g(x,t), g(x^{'},t^{'}))^2 = \nonumber\\ 2K(x,x^{'})^2K(t,t^{'})^2 
\end{align}. 

Note that this term is separable. 
It is now important to evaluate
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_integral}
&Cov \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{\max(t^{'},k(x^{'}))} h(z^{'}; t^{'}, k(x^{'})) g(x^{'},z^{'})^2 dz^{'}, \nonumber\\
&\qquad \int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k(x))} h(z; t, k(x)) g(x,z)^2 dz \bigg)
\end{align}.

Given that we are working with Gaussian Processes, $g(x,t)$ is sufficiently smooth and regular in the $t$ dimension such that we are able to interchange the Covariance operation and the integral. As such, we can rewrite $\eqref{eq:cov_integral}$ using Fubini's Theorem, and making a key substitution from the result in \eqref{eq:cov_3} yields that 

{\small
\begin{align}
\label{eq:cov_integral_interchange}
&Cov \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{\max(t^{'},k(x^{'}))} h\, g(x^{'},z^{'})^2 dz^{'}, \int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k(x))} h\, g(x,z)^2 dz \bigg) = \nonumber \\
&\iint h(z^{'}; t^{'}, k(x^{'})) h(z; t, k(x))\, E[g(x,z)^2 g(x^{'},z^{'})^2] \, dz \, dz^{'} \nonumber \\
&\quad - \int h(z; t, k(x)) E[g(x,z)^2]\,dz
    \cdot \int h(z^{'}; t^{'}, k(x^{'})) E[g(x^{'},z)^2]\,dz \nonumber \\
&= 2K(x,x^{'})^2 \iint h(z; t, k(x))\, h(z^{'}; t^{'}, k(x^{'}))\, K(z,z^{'})^2 \, dz \, dz^{'} \nonumber
\end{align}
}

Note however that the bounds of the integral are dependent on a function of both $t, x$. As a result, the covariance of this process is not separable.


\subsubsection{Expectation}
We will assume the formulation presented in \ref{sec:proposed_model}, where the maximum location $t_{max}$ and maximum value $f_{max}$ are specified. 
Thus,
\begin{align}
    E[f(x,t) | t_{max}(x), f_{max}(x)] & = f_{max}(x) +  \\ & \cdot(t-t_{max}(x))\int_{t_0}^{t_{max}(x)}E[g(x,z)^2 | x,z]dz  \nonumber \\ & -\int_{t}^{t_{max}(x)}  \int_{t_0}^s E[g(x,z)^2 | x,z]dzds \nonumber
\end{align}
Noting that our assumption was that $g(x,t)$ is a zero-mean GP with separable covariance in $x$ and $t$, $E[g(x,z)^2|x,z] = Var[g(x,z)|x,z] = \sigma^2_x\sigma^2_t = \sigma^2$. 
Thus, 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:expectation}
    E[f(x,t)|t_{max}(x), f_{max}(x)] & = f_{max}(x) + \sigma^2(t_{max}(x) - t_0)\cdot(t-t_{max}(x)) \nonumber \\ & -\sigma^2 \frac{(t_{max}(x)-t)^2}{2} 
\end{align}

If given $x$, $t_{max}(x) \sim Unif[t_0 = -L, L]$, we can compute the full expectation from \eqref{eq:expectation}.

\begin{align}
    E[f(x,t)] = f_{max}(x)  - \frac{\sigma^2 L^2}{2} + \sigma^2 Lt -  \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2} = f_{max}(x) - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}(t - L)^2
\end{align}
\subsubsection{Variance}
The law of iterated variance suggests that computing the full variance of our process involves computing $Var[f(x,t) | t_{max}, f_{max}]$, and $E[f(x,t) | t_{max}, f_{max}]$. We have already computed the latter in the previous section. 

\begin{align}
    \label{eq:variance_process}
    Var[f(x,t) | t_{max}(x), f_{max}(x)] & = \nonumber \\ & Var\bigg[ (t - t_{max}(x)) \cdot \int_{t_0}^{t_{max}(x)} g(x,z)^2 dz - \nonumber \\ & \int_{t}^{t_{max}(x)} \int_{t_0}^s g(x,z)^2 dzds \bigg] 
\end{align}.

Now, it remains to simplify the variance in \eqref{eq:variance_process}.
For notational brevity, let $t_{max}(x) = k$. We will start by first simplifying the integral sum, and by assuming that $t \leq k$.
\begin{align}
    \int_{t_0}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz - \int_t^k \int_{t_0}^s g(x,z)^2 dz ds \nonumber = \\
    \int_{t_0}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz - \int_{t_0}^k \int_{max(t,z)}^k g(x,z)^2dsdz = \nonumber \\
    \int_{t_0}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_{t_0}^t (t - k) g(x,z)^2dz - \int_{t}^k (k -z) g(x,z)^2dz  = \nonumber \\
     2 \int_{t_0}^t (t - k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_{t}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_{t}^k (k -z) g(x,z)^2dz = \nonumber \\
      2 \int_{t_0}^t (t - k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_{t}^k (t-z) g(x,z)^2dz
\end{align}

Now, if $t \geq k$,

\begin{align}
    \int_{t_0}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz - \int_t^k \int_{t_0}^s g(x,z)^2 dz ds \nonumber = \\
    2 \int_{t_0}^k (t - k) g(x,z)^2dz + 
 \int_{k}^t (z-t) g(x,z)^2dz
\end{align}

Thus, combining both cases,

\begin{align}
\label{eq:final_integral}
    \int_{t_0}^k (t-k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_t^k \int_{t_0}^s g(x,z)^2 dz ds \nonumber = \\
    2 \int_{t_0}^{min(t,k)} (t - k) g(x,z)^2dz + \int_{min(k,t)}^{max(t,k)} (t-z) g(x,z)^2dz
\end{align}

Let's define the following to simplify our expression

\begin{align}
h(z; t, k) = 
    \begin{cases}
        2(t-k) \quad z \in [t_0, min(t,k)] \\
        (t-z) \quad z \in [min(t,k), max(t,k)] \\
        0 \quad o.w
    \end{cases}
\end{align}

Thus,
\begin{align}
    Var[f(x,t) | t_{max}(x), f_{max}(x)] & = Var[\int_{t_0}^{max(t,k)} h(z; t, k) g(x,z)^2 dz]
\end{align}


Now, in order to compute the variance of this term, we will resort to the definition.

\begin{align}
    Var[\int_{t_0}^{max(t,k)} h(z; t, k) g(x,z)^2 dz] = E[(\int_{t_0}^{max(t,k)} h(z; t, k) g(x,z)^2 dz)^2] - \nonumber\\ E[\int_{t_0}^{max(t,k)} h(z; t, k) g(x,z)^2 dz]^2
\end{align}

Again, for notational brevity, let $ b = max(k,t)$. 

We will start by computing the expectation.

\begin{align}
    E[\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t,k) g(x,z)^2dz] = \nonumber \\
    \int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k) E[g(x,z)^2]dz = \nonumber \\
    \sigma^2 \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k)dz \bigg)^2
\end{align}

Next, we will compute the second moment.

\begin{align}
    E[\bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t,k) g(x,z)^2dz\bigg)^2] = \nonumber \\
    E[\bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b}\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t,k)h(z^{'}; t, k)g(x,z^{'})^2 g(x,z)^2dzdz^{'}\bigg)] = \nonumber \\
    \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b}\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k)h(z^{'}; t, k) E[g(x,z^{'})^2 g(x,z)^2] dzdz^{'}\bigg) = \nonumber \\
    \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b}\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k)h(z^{'}; t, k) (\sigma^4 + 2 K(z,z^{'})^2) dzdz^{'}\bigg) = \nonumber \\
    [\sigma^2 \bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k)dz \bigg)]^2 + 2\bigg(\int_{t_0}^{b}\int_{t_0}^{b} h(z; t, k)h(z^{'}; t, k) K(z,z^{'})^2dzdz^{'}\bigg)
\end{align}

Thus, the total variance is 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:conditional_variance}
    &Var\!\left[\int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t, k) g(x,z)^2\,dz\right] = \nonumber\\
    &\quad 2\!\left(\int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k)}\!\int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t, k)\, h(z^{'}; t, k)\,K(z,z^{'})^2\,dz\,dz^{'}\right)
\end{align}

This is quite difficult evaluate since the integrand involves the square of a kernel, however we can bound this variance by noting that $K(z,z^{'}) \leq \sigma^2$. 

Re-evaluating \eqref{eq:conditional_variance}, 

\begin{align}
     Var\!\left[\int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t,k) g(x,z)^2\,dz\right] \leq 2 \sigma^4 \left[\int_{t_0}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t, k)\, dz\right]^2
\end{align}

Now, lets make the same assumption we made in the previous section, $k \sim Unif[t_0 = -L, L]$. 

We can now take the expectation of this conditional variance over $k$.

\begin{align}
    E_k[Var(Y(k))] &\leq 2\sigma^4\, E_k\!\left[\left(\int_{-L}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t, k)\, dz\right)^2\right] \nonumber\\
    &= \frac{1}{2L} \int_{-L}^L\left[\int_{-L}^{\max(t,k)} h(z; t, k)\, dz\right]^2 dk
\end{align}

Note that we have a random variable $k$, which renders the integral a random variable. 

Unfortunately, after much mathematics, we are left with

\begin{align}
\label{eq:expectation_k}
    E_k[Var(Y(k))] \leq \frac{\sigma^4}{L} \bigg[\int_{-L}^t [2(t-k)(k+L) +  \frac{(k-t)^2}{2}]^2 dk + \nonumber \\ \int_t^L [2(t-k)(t+L) - \frac{(k-t)^2}{2}]^2 dk \bigg] 
\end{align}

It remains to compute $Var_k[E[f(x,t) | f_{max}, k]$. 

Recall from \eqref{eq:expectation},

\begin{align}
\label{eq:expectation_simple}
     E[f(x,t)|k, f_{max}(x)] & = f_{max}(x) + \sigma^2(k + L)\cdot(t-k) \nonumber \\ & -\sigma^2 \frac{(k-t)^2}{2} 
\end{align}.

Through very tedious computation,

\begin{align}
\label{eq:variance_k}
    Var_k[ E[f(x,t)|k, f_{max}(x)]] = \sigma^4 \Bigg[
&\frac{9L^4}{20}
+ (-tL + t^2)L^2 \nonumber \\
&+ \frac{L^2}{3}\left(4t^2 - 4tL + L^2\right) \nonumber \\
&+ \left(tL - \frac{t^2}{2} \right)^2
- \left(tL - \frac{L^2}{2} - \frac{t^2}{2} \right)^2
\Bigg] 
\end{align}

Finally, we can bound the variance of our process as the sum of \eqref{eq:variance_k} and \eqref{eq:expectation_k} plus some constants regarding the expectation and variance of $f_{max}$, noting that $f_{max} ,t_{max}$ are independent.
