\section{Introduction}

Understanding how NBA player performance evolves with age is central to both roster construction and player development.  General managers must project whether a player’s production will hold over the life of a proposed contract --- the Brooklyn Nets’ 2013 trade for aging stars Paul Pierce and Kevin Garnett (35 and 37 years old), exchanging future draft picks and young players, illustrates the franchise-altering cost of misjudging career trajectories.  From the athlete’s perspective, knowing which skills decline earliest can guide training adjustments: guards may shift toward shooting and decision-making, while bigs may emphasize positioning over athleticism as their careers progress.  \textit{Production curve analysis} formalizes this challenge as inferring smooth trajectories $f_p(t)$ from sparse longitudinal observations across players \citep{wakim2014functional}. 



The bulk of the production curve literature models focus on modeling the evolution of a scalar summary of NBA performance \citep{page2007using, vaci2019large, wakim2014functional}, and ignore more nuanced multivariate nature of player performance across  shooting, rebounding, and composite performance measures.  Rating systems such as RAPTOR \citep[Robust Algorithm using Player Tracking and On/Off Ratings;][]{natesilver538_2015, natesilver538_2019} estimate a single summary score via nearest-neighbor averaging of similar historical players. They impose similarity through fixed feature comparisons and are susceptible to selection bias at career extremes.  \citet{vaci2019large} improve upon naive averages by fitting piecewise parametric curves with the peak location specified a priori, but the rigid functional form limits adaptability to diverse career shapes.  Methods that do incorporate player pooling either use categorical positional types \citep{page2007using} or pre-specified continuous similarity \citep{wakim2014functional, vinue2019forecasting}; in particular, \citet{vinue2019forecasting} represent each player as a convex combination of a fixed set of archetypoids \citep{vinue2015archetypoids}, constraining the representation to the convex hull of an a-priori determined archetype set.

Existing approaches do not account for the evolution of performance across multiple metrics. We address this gap with a Bayesian multi-task Gaussian Process Latent Variable Model (GPLVM) that jointly estimates concave production curves across over a dozen performance metrics and thousands of NBA players.  Each player is assigned a latent embedding inferred from the full metric profile; players with similar embeddings learn similar aging trajectories without relying on positional labels or hand-specified archetypes.  Concavity is enforced through a novel nonparametric prior (the Concave Process) derived analytically from an HSGP approximation.  A Gompertz survival model accounts for right-censored careers, correcting the survivor bias that afflicts conditioning on observed seasons alone.  Posterior analysis of the learned embedding then supports (a) latent-space nearest-neighbor retrieval of career-comparable players, (b) hierarchical archetype discovery, and (c) counterfactual career estimation for injury-affected players.

First, we derive a novel nonparametric prior over concave functions to model the aging process in athletes. Evidence across multiple sports shows that production rises during development and declines with age-related attrition \citep[see e.g.][]{vaci2019large}, motivating a concave mean model. This prior specification allows us to directly study variations in aging effects (e.g peak behavior) across players. 

We construct these priors by applying a continuous push-forward map to an HSGP-approximated Gaussian process.  The resulting prior assigns positive mass to every uniform neighborhood of any continuous concave function, and admits an analytic closed form that makes it tractable for large-scale Bayesian inference.

Second, we simultaneously model dependence across multiple players and multiple metrics.  We embed each player in a shared low-dimensional latent space and use this embedding to parameterize a family of metric-specific concave aging curves simultaneously.  By respecting the native sampling distributions of count and proportion data, the model avoids the Gaussianity assumptions that distort inference for low-minute players. We are also able to incorporate an explicit Gompertz career-duration model accounts for right-censored and left-truncated careers, correcting the survivor bias that arises when conditioning only on observed seasons.

% Covariate-anchored latent variable prior.  Standard GPLVMs place an uninformative prior $X_p \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_q)$ on the latent coordinates, yielding a posterior identified only up to orthogonal rotation.  We instead place a structured prior $X_p \sim \mathcal{N}(Z_p^\top W,\, \sigma_X^2\, I_q)$ that anchors each player's latent position toward a learned projection of exogenous covariates (draft standing, height, and positional role).  Because $W$ and $\sigma_X$ are inferred jointly with the rest of the model, it learns both which combination of physical and scouting signals best predicts latent position and how tightly that prior should constrain the embedding.  This provides a model-level resolution to rotational non-identifiability that complements the posterior Procrustes alignment of Section~\ref{sec:inference}, and renders the learned embedding interpretable in terms of observable player characteristics.

We use the results of our latent variable representation to characterize how similar players age across a wide variety of metrics; in doing so, we are able to provide a posterior distribution of career trajectories across a wide set of metrics for prospective athletes with limited data. Furthermore, we are able to provide an understanding of when different metrics peak, as well as partition these metrics into skill-based and athleticism-based metrics to provide a wholistic view of an athlete's aging profile. Finally, we are able to examine how a player moves through their aging profile, creating a characterization of athletes who take longer to develop vs. those whose decline is rapid.

\section{Data and Exploratory Analysis}

\label{sec:data}
In this work, we use data from \url{basketball-reference.com} \citep{bballref} scraped using the package \texttt{ballr} \citep{ballr}. Our data consists of approximately 2300 NBA players from years 1997 - 2026, from the ages of 18 - 38. The data can be naturally viewed as a  tensor of size $P$ by $T$ by $K$ where $P$ is the number of players, $T$ is the range of ages observed across players' careers, and $K$ is the number of production metric curves.   Values may be missing for a player at certain ages due to injury, retirement (right censoring), or late entry into the league (left-censoring). The full list of metrics is given in Table \ref{tab:metric_description}. Metric definitions follow \cite{terner2020modeling}. 


% \subsection{Player Correlation}
In order to analyze such a complex data set, it is important to take advantage of the structure in the data. There are primarily three sources of variation, which intuitively align with each of the modes of the data tensor. However, within each mode, there exist inherent correlation which arises from how the sport is played, and has developed.


First, there exists an inherent low-rank structure to player similarity, from a domain-knowledge perspective. Players tend to fall into one of three categories (Guards, Forwards, and Centers) which can account for variation in shooting tendencies (Guards tend to be able to shoot better and from further out), as well as defensive metrics (Centers and Forwards typically accrue more blocks, Guards with more steals). Although these positional categories are not quite accurate, they do suggest some sort of low dimensional structure to the player space \cite{chessa2023complex}.

In addition to a low rank structure among the players, there also exists some low rank structure in the metric space. For example, free throw rate is correlated with two-point field goal rate; those who attack the rim are more likely to get fouled.  Shooting metrics tend to be correlated with one another as well, and players who are good at offensive rebounding will also likely be good at rebounding on the defensive end (Figure~\ref{fig:metric_corr} in the Appendix).

Finally, there is also some substructure to how metrics evolve with time. According to the literature of skill acquisition and aging, certain skills tend to rise in proficiency over time, and stabilize, resulting in a later peak. Other traits, like athleticism, are much more affected by aging tend to peak earlier, and drop off more rapidly. Although metrics might not be neatly categorized into these boxes, Figure~\ref{fig:metric_corr} (Appendix) shows that, at least empirically, there is some substructure to the metric information. We see the top left corner with a block of athleticism related skills ranging from block rate to defensive rebounding rate, followed by a block of shooting related skills (three point field goal rate to free throw percentage).

Kobe Bryant's athleticism-based metrics (steals, offensive rebounds) peak early and decline steeply, while his shooting metrics peak later and remain relatively stable --- a pattern consistent with skill-based development outlasting athleticism across a long career. 

Dwight Howard, on the other hand, is quite a different player from Bryant, although both entered the NBA at 18. He is an athletic 6 foot 10 center, who is known for dominating defense and interior scoring, and not much for his shooting proficiency. First, Howard's decline in composite metrics such as OBPM, DBPM, and minutes per game begins quite early and appears quite correlated with his decline in athleticism metrics. Contrast this with Bryant, whose composite curves seem to correlate with his proficiency in the skill metrics.

The differences in profiles between different athletes motivate a modeling approach that takes advantage of the inherent sub-structure of the data to intelligently share information across each of the key dimensions --- player, metric, and time. 












\section{Proposed Model}

\label{sec:proposed_model}

Let $\mathcal{Y}$ be the $P \times T \times K$ tensor of metrics $y_{ptk}$ be the measured outcome for player $p$ at age $t$, in metric $k$.  We model data at a yearly resolution between the ages of 18 and 38 ($t \in \{18, ..., 38\})$ over the 17 metrics shown in Figure \ref{fig:empirical_production_players} ($k \in \{1, ..., 17\})$. 
$\mathcal{Y}_{ptk}$ is missing for all $k$ if player $p$ does not play age $t$, either due to injury or retirement.  $\Omega$ is a binary tensor of the same dimensionality as $\mathcal{Y}$ indicating missing entries.


% To account for dependence between players and metrics, we allow this parameter to depend on low-dimensional player-specific latent variables, $x_p$.  Players with similar latent values, $x_p$, will have more similar production curves across metrics.  Because $x_p$ is shared across all metrics for player $p$, this also induces dependence between metrics. 

To account for the evolution of performance over time, we construct an aging function $f_k(x_p,t)$ such that it is concave in $t$ for all $p$ and $k$. This aging function enters additively into a latent natural parameter $\mu_{ptk}(x_p)$, which represents the expected capability of player $p$ at age $t$ in metric $k$ given a player-specific latent embedding $x_p$. This parameter functions as a link-transformed conditional expectation across a number of distinct likelihood families, allowing us to model non-Gaussian data. We extend this GLM-like construction to a career duration survival model, which also models the natural parameters as a function of $x_p$.  In short, our model can be succinctly characterized via the following equations, 
%
\begin{align}
\mu_{ptk} &= f_k(X_p,t) + r^k_p(t) \\
y_{p t k} &\sim \mathcal{F}_k\left(g_k^{-1}\left(\mu_{p t k}\right), \phi_k, \omega_{p t k}\right)
\end{align}
%
where $\mathcal{F}_k$ denotes the conditional distribution family corresponding to metric $k$,  $g_k(\cdot)$ is a metric-specific link function mapping the conditional expectation to the linear predictor space.  $\phi_k$ represents optional metric-level dispersion or variance scale parameters and $\omega_{ptk}$ represents time-varying, observation-specific exposure weights or trial totals, such as minutes played or total field goal attempts. $f^k(X_p,t)$ captures aspects of performance that are predictable given the performance of similar players over time, where similarity is induced by $X_p$, a low-dimensional latent player embedding (Section~\ref{sec:player_similarity}). Whereas $r_p^k(t)$ reflects aspects of the player's true skill that are not predictable (e.g.\ due to injury, change of strategy or role).  These components are distinct from the sampling variability due to finite observations.

For a given player-metric, we model the natural parameter $\mu_{ptk}$  as the sum of a concave function $f^k(X_p,t)$, which captures aging-related changes in performance, and $r^k_p(t) \sim \mathrm{AR}(1)$, which reflects idiosyncratic deviations from the underlying aging trend. We model similarity in expected performance across players and metrics using player-specific latent variable, $X_p$.






In Subsection \ref{sec:likelihoods}, we describe the likelihood functions for each metric. In Subsection \ref{sec:player_similarity} we describe how we model similarity in structural variations from a shared low-dimensional latent space across players, which jointly maps each likelihood to a unified representation of career progression. Finally, 
in Section \ref{sec:concave_prior} we describe how we construct the concave process for modeling changes in production over time.  






% Models which treat the inputs to  Gaussian Processes as unknown are  referred to as a Gaussian Process Latent Variable Model (GPLVM) \cite{JMLR:v6:lawrence05a}. Traditionally, Gaussian Process regression is focused on predicting a response $y$ from a set of query points $X^{*}$, given a set of fixed input points $X$. Latent Variable GPs, however, assume that the input points $X$ are not observed, and are rather a latent variable that is to be learned. With a linear kernel used in this paper, this approach resembles that of probabilistic PCA \cite{JMLR:v6:lawrence05a}. For a full treatment on GPLVMs, please consult \cite{bodin2017latentgaussianprocessregression}.

We therefore augment the trajectory likelihood with an explicit model for career duration that accounts for right-censored observations.


We model the  \emph{exit age} (e.g. retirement) of player $p$ using a survival model.  Let $T_p$ be the exit age and let $\alpha_p$ denote their \emph{entrance age}.  We place a player-specific \emph{Gompertz} proportional-hazards model on the time at risk $T_p \mid T_p > \alpha_p$, with age-varying hazard
\begin{equation}
    h_p(a \mid X_p) \;=\; \eta_p(X_p)\,\exp\!\bigl(\gamma_p(X_p)\,a\bigr),
    \label{eq:gompertz}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_p(X_p) > 0$ is the \emph{baseline hazard} and $\gamma_p(X_p) > 0$ is the \emph{aging rate} governing how quickly exit risk accelerates with age.  The Gompertz hazard is monotonically increasing in $a$ for any $\gamma_p > 0$, so the model encodes age-related attrition by construction; both parameters are tied to the latent embedding through the same feature-map projection $\phi(X_p)$ used by the performance model --- the identity map for the linear kernel, or random Fourier features for the flagship model (Appendix~\ref{sec:survival_linear_map}).
We adopt the Gompertz form because its log-hazard is linear in age, $\log h_p(a) = \log\eta_p(X_p) + \gamma_p(X_p)\,a$: exit risk increases by a constant proportional factor with each additional year of age.  This exponential-in-age hazard is the classical law of human aging and mortality and is the hallmark of biological senescence, making it a mechanistically faithful and interpretable model of athletic decline.

\subsection{Player Specific Latent Variables}
\label{sec:player_similarity}

A key challenge of our approach is ensuring a dependence between players and metrics. While we could use player-specific covariates to inform similarity between individuals (e.g. anthromporphic measurements, college performance, etc) we instead choose to model similarity between players in a data-informed way by treated player-specific covariates as latent variables. We borrow heavily from the methodology of Gaussian Process Latent Variable Models (GPLVMs) \cite{JMLR:v6:lawrence05a}, which treat the input $X$ as an unobserved latent variable. We introduce a $q$-dimensional latent variable $X_p \in \mathbb{R}^q$ for each player $p$, which we treat as an unknown and infer from the data. Rather than placing the traditional GPLVM isotropic prior on $X_p$, we encode prior domain knowledge through a \emph{structured} prior mean, by shrinking latent variables towards a linear combination of exogenous player attributes. We include height, draft position, and nominal position (Center, Guard, Forward). The prior mean pulls a player's latent position toward the subspace predicted by draft standing, physical size, and positional role; this allows latent representations for players with limited data to be shrunk towards a reasonable location in the latent space. 

\subsection{A Non-Parametric Prior for Concave Functions}
\label{sec:concave_prior}
 In addition to respecting well-studied aging processes, enforcing concavity also serves as a necessary regularizer against selection effects: cross-sectional averages at late ages (35+) are computed only over players still active in the league, who are disproportionately the better performing athletes.  Figure~\ref{fig:empirical_production} (Appendix) illustrates this directly: several metrics display an apparent uptick at older ages that violates the expected decline, an artifact of only high-performing players surviving to those ages.  A flexible unconstrained model would spuriously interpret this upward bias as a genuine late-career improvement.  The concave prior prevents the model from fitting this artifact as a real trajectory feature, playing a complementary role to the survival model: the Gompertz survival component accounts for which players we observe at all, while the concave prior constrains the shape of what we fit to those we do observe. 
 
We call this prior the ``concave process prior''. This process is the result of a composition of continuous push-forward maps applied to a Gaussian Process with a stationary kernel. Our approach is inspired the strategy used by \cite{convexityconstraints}, who propose a non-parametric prior over the space of monotonic functions.
The prior is placed on the latent function $f^k(X_p,t)$ in the linear predictor space and can be re-formulated to allow for more control of the peak behavior of the concave functions as well as allow covariate effects to modulate concave behavior. 

% \subsubsection{Concave Prior Construction}

\begin{proposition}[Concave Gaussian process prior]
Define the random function $h(t)$ as 
\begin{align}
    h(t) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 (t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s [g(z)]^2 \, dz \, ds,
    \label{eqn:concave_process_uni}
\end{align}
with arbitrary constants $\beta_0, \beta_1 \in \mathbb{R}$, where $g(z)$ is a Gaussian process.
Then $h(t)$ is almost surely concave, and the push-forward measure
\begin{align*}
\nu(A) &= \mu(T^{-1}(A)) \quad \text{for measurable } A, \\
T(g) &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 (t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s g(z)^2\, dz\, ds,
\end{align*}
puts positive mass on every uniform neighborhood of any continuous concave function.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
See Appendix \ref{appendix:convexity_proof}
\end{proof}
 


\begin{definition}[Concave Process Prior] 
A function h(t) has a \textbf{Concave Process (CP)} prior iff it has the form \ref{eqn:concave_process_uni}. We denote it as 
\begin{align}
    h(t) \sim \mathcal{CP}(\Theta)
\end{align}
where $\Theta$ indicate the parameters for $g(z)$, the underlying Gaussian Process. 

\end{definition}




In practice, the double integral in Equation \ref{eqn:concave_process_uni} cannot be evaluated analytically, so we make a linear approximation to the Gaussian process \citet{convexityconstraints}.  Specifically, we use the Hilbert Space GP basis function approximation (HSGP) \cite{Solin_2019}. 
This approximation converges as the number of basis functions goes to infinity, and allows us derive an analytic solution to the double integral and express $h(t)$ in a tractable form (Appendix \ref{appendix:HSGPs}). 

% We show in \ref{prop:hsgp_convergence_proof} that this approximation converges.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:concave_uni_gp}

Assume \eqref{eqn:concave_process_uni} holds and replace $g(t) \approx \sum_{l=1}^J S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l \psi_l(t)$, where $\psi_l(t)$ are the $J$ HSGP basis functions for a stationary kernel on $t$. Then, 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:final_uni_gp}
    f^{(k)}(t | \Theta) = a + b(t+L) - \mathbf{\alpha}^T \Psi(t) \mathbf{\alpha}
\end{align} 
%
where 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:phi}
\Psi_{lz}(t) = 
\begin{cases} \frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})^2} +
\frac{(t+L)^2}{4L}  & \text{if } l = z \\
\frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})^2} - \frac{cos(\epsilon_{lz}^{-}(t+L)) - 1}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{-})^2} & \text{if } l \neq z
\end{cases}
\end{align}
Here, $\mathbf{\alpha}$ is a $J$-vector with entries given by $\alpha_l \cdot S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2}$, and $\epsilon_{lz}^{+} = \sqrt{\lambda_l} + \sqrt{\lambda_z}$, $\epsilon_{lz}^{-} = \sqrt{\lambda_l} - \sqrt{\lambda_z}$, respectively. $\Theta$ indicates parameters relevant for the HSGP approximation, namely the lengthscale and variance parameters, $\omega$ and $s$. 

\begin{proof}
    see Appendix \ref{appendix:univariate_concavity}.
\end{proof}

\end{proposition}






\subsubsection{Reparameterizing the Concave Process}
The concave process, Equation \eqref{eq:final_uni_gp} can be reparameterized into an interpretable form that is more amenable to prior elicitation.  Specifically, note that $a$ and $b$ can be modeled as variationally independent parameters that control the shape of the concave function. For modeling NBA production, we are most interesting in reasoning about both the value and age of peak performance. As such, define $f^*$ and $t^*$ the max and argmax of $f(t)$ respectively, that is

\begin{align}
f^* = \underset{t}{\text{max }} f(t) = f(t^*) 
\end{align}
%
In the following Theorem, we state the parameterization of the concave process in terms of $f^*$ and $t^*$.
%
\begin{theorem}\label{theorem:concave_max_approx}
Let $f(t | \Theta)$
%\approx D(x) + C(x)(t+L) -  \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T\mathbf{A}^T \Psi(t) \mathbf{\tilde{A}} \mathbf{\phi}(x)$ 
be defined as in Equation \eqref{eq:final_uni_gp}. Then, if $f(t | \Theta)$ takes on the maximum value of $f^*$ at time $t^*$,  

\begin{align}
\label{eq:max_constraint_gp}
    f^{(k)}(t | \Theta) = f^* + \mathbf{A}^T [ \Psi(t^*) - \Psi(t) + \frac{d\Psi(t^*)}{dt}(t - t^*) ] \mathbf{A}  
\end{align}

where $\frac{d\Psi(t^*)}{dt}$, the time derivative of \eqref{eq:phi}, can be computed analytically.
% \begin{align}
% \label{eq:phi_prime}
% \Psi^{'}_{lz}(t) = 
% \begin{cases} \frac{sin(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L))}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})} +
% \frac{(t+L)}{2L}  & \text{if } l = z \\
% \frac{sin(\epsilon_{lz}^{+}(t+L))}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{+})} - \frac{sin(\epsilon_{lz}^{-}(t+L))}{2L(\epsilon_{lz}^{-})} & \text{if } l \neq z
% \end{cases}
% \end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
See Appendix \ref{appendix:peak_value}.
\end{proof}



Because all link functions used in this paper (exponential for Poisson and Negative-Binomial metrics; logistic for Binomial and Beta-Binomial) are strictly monotone increasing, the location of the maximum is preserved under the inverse link: $\arg\max_t \, g^{-1}(f(t)) = \arg\max_t \, f(t)$.  Consequently, the peak age $t^*_k$ and the rank ordering of peak values are exactly preserved in the observable space, and the model's ability to place a structured prior on peak age and peak value is maintained regardless of observation family.


\subsubsection{Modeling Dependence Across Concave Processes}

In this Section, we extend the univariate concave process to a process over multiple concave functions.  Here, we assume each process is associated with auxiliary time-invariant covariates, $x$.  We let $h(x,t)$  denote a multivariate concave process associated with covariate $x$ which is concave in $t$ and model the dependence between $h(x,t)$ and $h(x',t)$ for $x \neq x'$. We extend the concave process to a multivariate processes by using a multi-output Gaussian process. 


%
% We construct this prior by noting that the following function $f(x,t)$ is concave in $t$ if and only if $\frac{\partial ^2 f(x,z)}{\partial z^2} \geq 0$.

% \begin{align}
% \label{eq:concave_multi_rep}
%     f(x,t) = a(x) + b(x)(t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s \frac{[g(x,z)]^2}dzds 
% \end{align}
%
Specifically, we can construct concave functions, $f(x,t)$, by extending \eqref{eq:final_uni_gp} by integrating the square of a multi-input Gaussian Process, $g(x,t)$ and allowing the integration constants $a$ and $b$ to also depend on $x$. Specifically, $g(x,t) \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, K_x \otimes K_t)$, where $K_x$ and $K_t$ are stationary covariance kernels acting on the $x$ space, and time $t$, independently. 
We wish to find all set of $f(x,t)$ such that $[g(x,t)] ^2 = \frac{\partial ^2 f(x,t)}{\partial t^2} \geq 0$. As such, our desired function has the form
\begin{align}
\label{eq:concave_multi_rep}
    h(x,t) = a(x) + b(x)(t-t_0) - \int_{t_0}^t \int_{t_0}^s [g(x,z)]^2dzds 
\end{align}
where $a(x), b(x)$ are some constants of integration with respect to $t$.

\begin{definition}[Multivariate Concave Process Prior] 
A function h(x, t) has a \textbf{Multivariate Concave Process (MCP)} prior iff it has the form \ref{eq:concave_multi_rep}. We denote it as 
\begin{align}
    h(x, t) \sim \mathcal{MCP}(\Theta; X)
\end{align}
where $\Theta$ denotes parameters for the underlying Gaussian process $g(x,z)$, and $X$ indicates the additional input dimensions.
\end{definition}


% \newtheorem{prop}{Proposition}
\begingroup\sloppy
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:concave_multi_gp}
Assume \eqref{eq:concave_multi_rep} holds and let $g(x,t) = \sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x) \bigl( \sum_{l=1}^K S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l^i \psi_l(t)\bigr)$, where $\phi_i(x)$ is a projection of $x$ such that $\phi(x)^T \phi(x) \approx \mathcal{K}(x,x')$ and $\psi_l(t)$ are HSGP basis functions for $t$. Then,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:final_gp}
    f^{(k,m)}(x,t| \Theta) &= f^{*}(x) \nonumber\\
    &+  \mathbf{\phi}(x)^T\mathbf{A}^T \!\left[ \Psi(t^{*}(x)) - \Psi(t) + \tfrac{d\Psi(t^*(x))}{dt} (t - t^{*}(x)) \right] \mathbf{A}\, \mathbf{\phi}(x)
\end{align}
%
where $\Psi_{lz}(t)$ is defined as in Equation \ref{eq:phi}, and $\mathbf{A}$ is a $M$ by $K$ matrix where $\mathbf{A}_{il} = S(\sqrt{\lambda_l})^{1/2} \alpha_l^i$. Our formulation  of $f(x, t | \Theta) \xrightarrow{} h(x,t)$ uniformly  \ref{eq:concave_multi_rep}
as $K, M \xrightarrow{} \infty$.

\noindent Proof: see Appendix \ref{appendix:multivariate_concavity}.
\end{proposition}
\endgroup

Much like the univariate case in \ref{theorem:concave_max_approx},  parameters $f^{*}(x)$ and $t^{*}(x)$ can be specified as variationally independent processes, with the additional benefit that these parameters can now pool information across additional dimensions. As a result, we have now a process which can produce concavity across functions, while ensuring that these functions will behave similarly (peak value, peak location, etc.)  with respect to other input dimensions. Naturally, we introduce our latent variable $X_p$ into this formulation, allowing us to model multiple concave curves that encode dependence across metrics and players.
























\section{Inference and Hyperparameter Selection}
\label{sec:inference}

We implement our model in the probabilistic programming language \texttt{NumPyro} \cite{numpyro}. Full MCMC inference in this model is challenging Bayesian factor models typically have rotational identifiability issues with either the loadings or the factors themselves, yielding a multi-modal posterior. 


In particular, we find that MCMC sampler gets stuck at suboptimal modes when randomly initialized.  As such, to start the the sampler near a well-identified mode by initializing the MCMC chains from a maximum a posteriori estimate. The MAP estimate is used to fix a small set of kernel hyperparameters whose posterior is well-determined by the full data: specifically, the concave GP lengthscale and amplitude ($\alpha$, $l_k$, $\sigma_t$, $\sigma_c$), the structured prior scales ($\sigma_W$, $\sigma_X$), and the Gompertz global hazard offset parameters. All remaining parameters, including the latent embeddings $X_p$ for all players, the HSGP curve weights $\beta$, the per-metric peak parameters ($c_{\max}$, $t_{\max}^{\text{raw}}$), and the full AR(1) components ($\rho^k$, $\sigma^k_{\mathrm{AR}}$, $\mathbf{r}^k_p$) are sampled. 

The MAP estimation and MCMC sampling were conducted in \texttt{NumPyro} \citep{numpyro} with a GPU, with the MAP estimate obtained by Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) and MCMC samples acquired using No U-Turn Sampling (NUTS). Posterior samples were obtained from 4 chains, each running 2{,}000 post-warmup iterations (1{,}000 warmup) and thinned by a factor of 8, yielding 250 retained draws per chain (1{,}000 total); the injury variant uses 50 draws per chain (200 total) due to its higher computational cost. Effective sample size and  $\hat{R}$ diagnostics can be found in Appendix~\ref{sec:convergence}. We select the dimension of the latent player embedding, $q$, by conducting a holdout ablation study (Appendix~\ref{sec:ablation_q}).  A latent dimension of $q = 10$ was used for the player embedding space, along with 20 HSGP basis functions, to approximate the CPLVM. 

\paragraph{Procrustes alignment for Latent Space Posterior Analysis}

Because the latent embedding $X_p \in \mathbb{R}^q$ is only identified up to orthogonal rotation of its learned coordinates, raw MCMC draws from different chains occupy different rotation frames and cannot be directly compared or averaged. There are different approaches for dealing with nonidentifiability of factors across multiple MCMC samples \citep{procrustes, poworoznek2024efficientlyresolvingrotationalambiguity}. To enable coherent posterior summaries, we apply Procrustes alignment to each (chain, draw) pair: given the MAP estimate $\hat{X}$ as a fixed reference, we find the orthogonal matrix $R^{(s)}$ minimising $\|\hat{X} - X^{(s)} R^{(s)}\|_F$ via singular value decomposition, and replace each sampled $X^{(s)}$ with the aligned version $X^{(s)} R^{(s)}$ before any downstream computation.  All posterior quantities reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} --- nearest-neighbor tables, archetype clustering, and embedding uncertainty --- are derived from these Procrustes-aligned samples.  Convergence diagnostics for the aligned draws are shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:convergence}.


\paragraph{Posterior Predictive Generation}
Many metrics are conditional on each other through the exposure structure: counting statistics depend on minutes played, which in turn depends on games played.  Posterior predictive curves are therefore generated in three stages.  \textbf{Stage 1}: games played (GP\%) is sampled from its posterior predictive distribution.  \textbf{Stage 2}: minutes per game (MPG) is sampled conditionally, with the stage-1 games draw used to form the total-minutes exposure.  \textbf{Stage 3}: all counting and rate metrics (FTA, AST, DREB, etc.) are sampled using the composite minutes from stages 1--2 as their exposure offset; shooting percentages are then derived from the sampled counts.  This cascade propagates exposure uncertainty forward: if the stage-1 draw yields zero games, all downstream metrics are zero, encoding the possibility that the player has exited the league.  The model thereby integrates production uncertainty with career-length uncertainty within a single posterior draw, which is the primary operational value for front-office forecasting.

The athleticism group (green) --- offensive rebounding (OREB), steals (STL), blocked shots (BLK), free-throw attempts (FTA), and games played (GP\%) --- consists of metrics which rely predominantly on quickness, vertical jump ability, and strength. The skill group (red) collects the pure shooting metrics: free-throw percentage (FT\%), three-point percentage (FG3\%), and three-point attempt rate (FG3A). The remaining metrics form the "both" group (blue), which lies at the intersection of learned ability and athleticism: assists (AST), turnovers (TOV), offensive and defensive box plus/minus (OBPM, DBPM), minutes per game (MPG), usage (USG), defensive rebounding (DREB), and two-point shooting volume and efficiency (FG2A, FG2\%). Generating an assist, for example, requires the anticipatory court vision accrued over time coupled with the athletic ability required to deliver it.


To see how various metrics peak, we compute the 95 \% CI of samples, averaged over all players by metric. There is some pattern which reflects that metrics assigned by our previously mentioned grouping to be reliant on "athleticism"  tend to peak earlier, while those categorized as "skill" based metrics tend to peak later. This is showcased in Figure~\ref{fig:peaks}. All metrics seem to peak between the ages of 20 and 30, with the athleticism metrics peaking before 25. Most importantly, it appears as if skill based metrics seem to peak around 26-28; this suggests to NBA decision makers that if young players do not show significant improvement in these metrics by age 23-24, it is likely that they are already close to their peak value.

\subsection{Early Identification of Peak Performers and Draft Evaluation}
A central question for front offices is how quickly the model can resolve a player's peak age and peak value from limited early-career data.  Because $X_p$ is inferred from the full longitudinal metric profile and peak timing is encoded directly in the concave trajectory $f^k(X_p, t)$, the posterior over both peak age $t^*_{pk}$ and peak value $f^*_{pk}$ contracts as more seasons are observed.  For a rookie with one or two seasons of data, the posteriors are wide: the model registers genuine uncertainty about where the career arc will crest.  By the third or fourth season --- once above-replacement minutes accumulate and the trajectory's curvature becomes discernible --- the posteriors concentrate substantially.

The structured prior on $X_p$ (Equation~\ref{eq:structured_prior}) anchors this process: draft standing, height, and positional role provide an informative starting point, so that the model does not require five seasons of evidence before forming a view.  Posterior uncertainty has a direct front-office interpretation --- a wide credible region for $f^*_{pk}$ indicates that a player's ceiling is not yet determined by the data, while a narrow region indicates confident placement.  A breakout season shifts the posterior mean of $X_p$ substantially, whereas consistent-but-unremarkable production converges slowly. 

We operationalise this as a draft-cohort evaluation tool using four posterior summaries per player: posterior mean peak OBPM; $P(\text{OBPM}{>}2)$, $P(\text{OBPM}{>}3)$, and $P(\text{OBPM}{>}4)$ — the fraction of posterior peak-value draws exceeding each threshold; and expected years to peak, the posterior mean OBPM peak age minus the player's last observed age.  Empirically, a career-peak OBPM of 2 corresponds to roughly the 81st percentile of NBA players, 3 to the 88th, and 4 to the 93rd, so these thresholds span the range from reliable above-average starter to All-Star caliber production.  Table~\ref{tab:breakout_cohort} reports the top five players per entry cohort ranked by posterior mean peak OBPM.  Posterior confidence grows sharply with seasons observed: the 2021--2022 cohorts show high mean peak OBPM for their top players (Zion Williamson, Tyrese Haliburton, LaMelo Ball) with near-certain $P(\text{OBPM}{>}2)$, while the 2026 cohort, with only one season of data, yields more diffuse probabilities.  Victor Wembanyama tops the 2025 cohort, reflecting the model's rapid posterior contraction after an exceptional rookie season; his years-to-peak estimate is under one year (0.7), placing him at the leading edge of his projected prime.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced a joint Bayesian model for multi-metric NBA career trajectories that combines three novel components: a Concave Process (CP) prior over production curves that encodes the biological constraint of concave aging, a Gaussian process latent variable model (GPLVM) that embeds all 17 metrics into a shared low-dimensional player representation, and a survival-adjusted injury submodel that enables counterfactual inference about career arcs absent specific injury events.

% \paragraph{Key quantitative findings}
Under the holdout-last-$k$ evaluation scheme (727 player-seasons, 2022--2025), the Concave model achieves broadly comparable predictive performance to the AR baseline: by RMSE, the Concave prior is competitive or superior across all metrics; by ELPPD, the AR baseline retains an advantage on persistent rate statistics (USG\%, MPG, OBPM, DBPM) where its persistence assumption is naturally well-suited, while the Concave$+$AR model significantly outperforms on count statistics (BLK, STL, DREB, OREB; $|\Delta|>1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$).
The Concave$+$AR$+$Injury model achieves the best log-loss on AST (9.75 vs.\ 10.25 for Concave$+$AR) and FTA (8.58), confirming that injury structure primarily affects high-volume counting statistics.
The primary contribution beyond predictive parity is structural: the AR model cannot produce posterior estimates of peak age or peak value, embed players in a shared latent space, or support counterfactual injury inference --- capabilities that are orthogonal to any holdout comparison and constitute the core scientific offering of the paper.
The shared latent embedding supports principled player similarity retrieval: for Nikola Joki\'{c}, the GPLVM latent space recovers high-usage pass-first bigs (Arvydas and Domantas Sabonis, Giannis Antetokounmpo), while the Fr\'{e}chet curve distance recovers players whose aggregate trajectory shape matches Joki\'{c}'s regardless of position.
Peak-age PCA reveals interpretable metric groupings --- athleticism-driven metrics (OREB, BLK) peak before age 25, skill-based metrics (shooting efficiency, playmaking) peak around 26--28 --- providing actionable guidance for player development timelines.

% \paragraph{Limitations}
Several limitations should be noted.
First, the model is estimated on players who appeared in at least one NBA game, introducing survivorship bias: players who failed to reach or sustain NBA careers are unobserved, so the posteriors describe the distribution over NBA-caliber trajectories rather than the full talent distribution.
Second, the holdout coverage analysis reveals calibration heterogeneity across metrics (60--99\% vs.\ the nominal 95\%), with OBPM showing the largest shortfall; richer non-Gaussian likelihoods or heavier-tailed error models could improve calibration for the most volatile metrics.
Third, the injury causal estimates rest on the assumption of no unmeasured confounding conditional on latent player quality, observable covariates, and career history --- an assumption that is strong and fundamentally untestable.
Fourth, the injury counterfactual analysis conditions on the first observed major injury and selects only players who returned to play afterward; players who retired immediately after injury are excluded.  This induces immortal-time bias: the estimated effects describe the subset of players robust enough to return and may understate the total career impact of the most severe injuries.
Finally, the current GP latent variable model uses a linear kernel ($\phi(X_p) = X_p$), which may not capture nonlinear interactions between player archetypes and metric trajectories.

\paragraph{Future directions}
Several extensions are natural.
The most immediate is to apply the framework to other professional sports leagues where longitudinal performance data are available (e.g., soccer, hockey, baseball), testing whether the concave aging prior and GPLVM structure generalize beyond basketball.
A richer injury submodel --- for example, one that explicitly models injury severity, recovery time, and metric-specific damage profiles --- could improve counterfactual precision and support more granular roster management decisions.
On the inference side, the current MAP-initialized MCMC approach is computationally intensive; variational approximations or normalizing-flow posteriors for the latent space could enable real-time player evaluations as new season data arrive.
Finally, integrating the trajectory model with contract-length and salary-cap data would allow front offices to assess not just athletic value but economic value over the duration of a proposed contract.
